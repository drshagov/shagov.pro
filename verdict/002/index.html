VERDICT  002  Infusions

Can a drip slow aging?

Verdict issued 8 Nov 2026 Full review published 13 Nov 2026 Evidence last reviewed 8 Nov 2026
VerdictWhat to doEvidenceSafetySources
SHAGOV  |  VERDICT.Not justified.Issued · 13.11.2026

IV NAD+ has not been shown to slow aging in healthy adults. On current evidence, routine commercial infusions for this purpose are not justified. This is not a finding that NAD+ is ineffective for every medical use.

The claim we tested
A course of IV NAD+ can slow aging in an otherwise healthy adult. The promise often comes with attractive extras: more cellular energy, better DNA repair, sharper thinking. But how much of that promise survives clinical testing?
What this verdict does not mean

That NAD+ cannot help patients with specific diseases. Some clinical findings deserve further investigation.

That physicians who prescribe it are acting in bad faith. This review does not judge their motives or professional integrity.

Not justified does not mean proven ineffective. This verdict concerns one specific anti-aging claim. It is dated and will be reconsidered when stronger clinical evidence becomes available.

Author's disclosure ↓

Take this with you

What to do instead

You came looking for a way to slow aging. Here is what the evidence actually supports, and what to ask before you pay for a drip.

The measures with the strongest evidence for a longer, healthier life are not new: regular physical activity, controlled blood pressure, not smoking. Large studies show meaningful health benefits from these measures. For IV NAD+ as an anti-aging intervention in healthy adults, we have no comparable evidence.

If a drip takes the time, money or attention you would otherwise give to these, it costs more than its price.

Three questions before you pay
  1. What exactly should change, by when, and how will we check it?No measurable goal and no follow-up measurement means nothing can be verified.
  2. Where is the study?Ask for the authors, journal, year, and a DOI or registry number. A journal name alone is not enough. Marketing pages describe a randomized IV NAD+ trial in Nature Aging that took 2.4 years off biological age. It lists no authors, no DOI and no registry number. We could not find it in the journal or in trial registries.
  3. What exactly goes into my vein?Ask who made the product, the batch number, where the ingredient came from, and whether it was tested for sterility and endotoxins. The FDA has documented a contaminated NAD+ batch: three patients who received it were directed to the emergency room.
01 · The Biology

Why NAD+ matters — and why more isn't necessarily better.

NAD+ is not just another molecule marketed for longevity. It is essential to life.

Together with NADH, it participates in reactions that make cellular energy metabolism possible. It is also used by enzymes involved in DNA repair, cellular regulation and stress responses — including PARPs and sirtuins.

This is established biology. And it explains why scientists have spent decades studying NAD+.

The body does not have one universal NAD+ reservoir. Its metabolism differs between blood, muscles, brain and other tissues — and even between compartments within the same cell. (Nikiforov et al., 2011)

A higher concentration in blood does not automatically mean more NAD+ where it is needed. And even a higher concentration inside a cell does not automatically mean better function.

The question is not whether our cells need NAD+. They do.

The question is whether giving more NAD+ intravenously makes healthy humans function better — or age more slowly.

That is where the clinical evidence must take over from the biological theory.

Check 1 · Premise

The pitchNAD+ declines with age.

The check: Not everywhere.

In blood

Two studies published in Nature Metabolism in 2026 used validated methods and found no consistent age-related decline in healthy adults.

The first measured NAD+ in whole blood in 303 people from seven cohorts. Levels did not change with age, diet or exercise. They did rise after people took nicotinamide riboside, so the method could detect a real increase. The authors explain earlier conflicting results by sample handling: freezing blood without preservation destroys much of the NAD+. (Trętowicz et al., 2026)

The second measured total NAD (NAD+ plus NADH) in 1,068 adults in Lithuania. It was stable in healthy men and rose with age in healthy women. It was lower in people with cardiovascular disease and high blood pressure. The design was cross-sectional, so it cannot show cause and effect. The authors state that their data do not contradict declines in tissues and do not show that supplements help. (Romenskaja-Avgul et al., 2026)

The two studies measured different things and reached consistent conclusions.

Not every study agrees. An earlier Chinese cohort of 1,518 adults without heart disease or cancer found that whole-blood NAD+ declined with age in men, but not in women. (Yang et al., 2022) Differences in sample handling may contribute to conflicting findings, but the discrepancy is not fully resolved.

Blood is also not the whole body. As an accompanying commentary notes, it gives an incomplete picture of NAD metabolism in tissues. (Gardell & Baur, 2026)

In tissues

Muscle. In biopsies from 52 people, NAD+ was among the metabolites most reduced in older adults and was lowest in those with functional limitations. In older adults it tracked mitochondrial function and daily steps. The study was cross-sectional: it cannot show that low NAD+ causes decline, or that activity restores it. (Janssens et al., 2022)

Brain. Two small imaging studies at 7 tesla found lower NAD+ with age in one region of the brain, the occipital cortex. (Zhu et al., 2015; Bagga et al., 2020) The groups were small, and the findings cannot be extended to the whole brain.

Skin. In 49 skin samples, NAD+ was inversely related to age and oxidative damage. (Massudi et al., 2012)

A 2025 review concluded that age-related NAD+ decline in humans has been robustly shown in only a limited number of studies, and not in every tissue. (Vinten et al., 2025)

Cause or consequence?

Unknown. In human tissues, lower NAD+ could drive dysfunction, result from it, or simply accompany it.

There is also no clinical threshold for an "age-related NAD+ deficiency" that would call for replacement, let alone by IV. Medicine does recognize niacin deficiency: pellagra, caused by inadequate vitamin B3 or tryptophan. It is treated with oral niacin or nicotinamide. But that is not the same as a clinically defined age-related NAD+ deficiency.

What this means

NAD+ falls with age in some tissues, in small studies. In the blood of healthy adults, a universal decline is not confirmed. Its causal role in human aging is not established.

Evidence quality: mixed. For causation: low.

Check 2 · Delivery

The pitchAn IV drip restores it.

The check: Plasma NAD+ can rise. Delivery to target tissues remains unverified.

What was measured in people

In a small human pilot study, eight men aged 30 to 55 received 750 mg of NAD+ over six hours, and three received saline. For the first two hours, plasma NAD+ did not rise significantly, suggesting rapid removal from circulation through uptake, metabolism, or both. By six hours, plasma NAD+ had risen by 398%, about fivefold, and it remained elevated at eight hours. No adverse reactions were reported during the infusion. Only plasma and urine were measured, not blood cells or tissues; the authors name this as a gap. (Grant et al., 2019)

Plasma NAD+: +398% vs baseline at 6 hours · schematic · Grant et al., 2019

A second pilot, published as a preprint without peer review, gave 500 mg of NAD+ IV and measured NAD+ in whole blood. The changes were small and not statistically significant: +2% at 24 hours, +8.8% at 7 days, +15.1% at 14 days. (Hawkins et al., 2024)

The two studies do not contradict each other. They used different doses, protocols and measurements: plasma in one, whole blood in the other.

What this means

An IV drip can sharply raise NAD+ in plasma. The body has well-described ways to break NAD+ down and rebuild it inside cells, but how much each route contributes after an infusion in humans is unknown. No study has directly measured whether IV NAD+ raises NAD+ levels in human muscle, brain or other target tissues.

Blood is not muscle. Blood is not brain.

"Not measured" does not mean "does not happen". It means the commercial explanation has a gap here. And a gap in the mechanism is not, by itself, an argument against the drip. The decisive question is whether people actually benefit. That is the next check.

Evidence quality: plasma levels — direct but limited human data. Breakdown and rebuilding outside and inside cells — strong laboratory evidence. Delivery to target tissues after IV NAD+ — not confirmed in humans.

02 · The Human Studies

Trial results, doses and limitations.

Check 3 · Function

The pitchCells work better.

The check: No convincing clinical evidence in healthy adults. Preliminary positive findings exist in heart disease, at much lower doses.

We looked at three levels: NAD+ inside cells, biological pathways, and how people actually feel and function.

Inside cells

No study has directly measured NAD+ in human target tissues after an IV drip (see Check 2).

Biological pathways

A conference abstract of about 200 words described eight healthy adults aged 70 to 80 who received 1,000 mg of NAD+ IV repeatedly over six days. It reported changes in the blood NAD metabolome, in sirtuin-related markers in blood immune cells, and in markers of oxidative stress and inflammation. The abstract reports significant biological changes, but provides no detailed statistical methods or results. Without a control group or a full paper, these findings remain hypothesis-generating. These are cells from blood, not tissues. (Braidy, 2018)

In a heart failure trial, a much lower dose shifted some blood markers linked to sirtuins. (Pei et al., 2024; details below)

Human function in healthy adults

Studies in healthy adults do exist. They tested blood levels and tolerability, not whether people age more slowly or feel better.

In a retrospective review of medical records from a commercial wellness network, six people received 500 mg of NAD+ IV on four consecutive days and were followed for 30 days. Questionnaires on quality of life, fatigue, thinking and sleep, along with wearable ring data, were collected, but their results were not published. (Reyna et al., 2026)

The preprint pilot above listed sleep and energy scales in its methods, but did not report a comparison of these outcomes against saline. (Hawkins et al., 2024)

A 2026 systematic review found no studies of IV or intramuscular NAD+ that met its criteria for anti-aging and wellness outcomes. (Gallagher & Emmanuel, 2026)

Bottom line for healthy adults: no convincing controlled evidence that an IV drip improves energy, thinking or physical performance.

Heart disease: the strongest positive findings

Three randomized trials from China tested IV NAD+ in heart patients. We do not hide them behind a red verdict.

Older adults with heart failure

58 analyzed, 50 mg IV daily for 7 days against saline, double-blind. There was no significant difference between groups in heart pumping function. A heart-strain marker in the blood, NT-proBNP, showed a significant difference only in an analysis of change over time, not at individual visits. One-year survival was 96.6% against 89.7%, not statistically significant. The groups differed at baseline: high blood pressure was more common in the control group, which adds uncertainty in a trial of this size. (Pei et al., 2024)

Heart failure after coronary disease

180 patients, 10 mg IV daily for 7 days against placebo, on top of standard treatment. At one month, heart pumping function (ejection fraction) was 45.4% against 42.4%, a significant difference. Major cardiac events over six months: 14.6% against 24.7%, not statistically significant. (Yu et al., 2026, heart failure)

After a heart attack with stent placement

120 patients, 10 mg IV daily for 7 days against placebo. At 30 days, ejection fraction was 56.3% against 53.4%, p = 0.048. Major events over six months were not significantly reduced. The published change scores do not fully match the published group averages: a question about reporting, not evidence of misconduct. (Yu et al., 2026, heart attack)

What limits these findings

  • These are patients with heart disease, not healthy adults.
  • The doses were 10–50 mg, versus 500–1,000 mg in many commercial wellness drips — a tenfold to hundredfold difference. Neither efficacy nor safety can be assumed to carry over.
  • The main positive result is heart pumping function, a measurement, not fewer heart attacks or deaths.
  • Two of the three trials come from one research group, and none has been independently replicated.
  • Funding and drug supply are detailed in The Money Behind the Science.

Different patients. Different goals. Different risks.

What oral precursors teach us

Pills that raise NAD+, nicotinamide riboside and NMN, are not IV NAD+, and their results do not test the drip. But they show one thing clearly: raising NAD+ does not guarantee a functional benefit.

  • A meta-analysis found no support for NMN or NR in preserving muscle mass and function in people over 60. (Prokopidis et al., 2025)
  • In long COVID, NR raised NAD+ but did not improve thinking, fatigue, sleep or mood compared with placebo. (Wu et al., 2025)
  • In mild cognitive impairment, NR roughly doubled blood NAD+, without significant improvement in cognition. (Martens et al., 2025)
  • In peripheral artery disease, NR improved the six-minute walk distance by 17.6 meters compared with placebo, a positive signal that needs confirmation. (McDermott et al., 2024)

Results depend on the disease, the population and the outcome.

Why some people feel better after a drip

Some people report feeling better after a drip. Could that be NAD+ itself? Perhaps. But expectations, time spent resting, attention from staff and the administered fluid may also contribute. Without a well-designed comparison, we cannot tell how much of the experience comes from NAD+.

What this means

The human studies tell two different stories.

In healthy adults, IV NAD+ has been studied mainly for blood levels and tolerability. Some studies collected measures of well-being, but convincing evidence of improved energy, cognition or physical performance is missing.

In patients with heart disease, small randomized trials have reported preliminary improvements in cardiac function. These results deserve further investigation.

But a better measurement of heart function in a sick patient is not proof of slower aging in a healthy person.

Different patients. Different goals. Different risks.

Evidence quality: insufficient for wellness benefits; preliminary and limited for specific cardiac outcomes.

The twist

In China, NAD+ is a medicine.

As a drugYes

Coenzyme I for Injection has been marketed in China since 2002 for specific indications, including adjunctive treatment of coronary heart disease. (Knature Biopharmaceutical, 2026) According to a September 2026 CCTV report, the described approved regimen is an intramuscular injection of 5 mg. The cardiac trials above evaluated experimental IV regimens of Coenzyme I for Injection.

To slow agingNo

The same report states that China has never approved any drug for an anti-aging indication. It called the NAD+ "youth shot" a gimmick. (CCTV News, 2026)

Medical use ≠ anti-aging proof.

Check 4 · Outcome

The pitchAging slows down.

The check: This is where the promise breaks.

0eligible controlled trials of IV NAD+ demonstrating slower aging in healthy adults. Based on the 2026 systematic review, which searched literature through October 2025, and our targeted review of subsequent evidence. (Gallagher & Emmanuel, 2026) The one widely shared "trial" that claims to show this could not be verified (see Three questions before you pay).

A trial to watch

A registered trial in Shanghai plans to test seven days of IV Coenzyme I against saline in 60 adults aged 40–70 with increased arterial stiffness and high blood pressure.

Its primary outcome is endothelial function, measured by flow-mediated dilation. Arterial stiffness is a secondary outcome.

The registry lists June 2027 as the estimated primary completion date, but the latest verified record, from January 2026, still showed that recruitment had not begun. (NCT07328100)

Even a positive result would not, by itself, demonstrate slower aging across the body.

The limits of this review. This is a targeted audit of the sources, not an exhaustive systematic review of every database, including Chinese, Japanese and Korean ones. Our ratings of evidence quality are editorial judgments, not a completed formal grading.
What this means

In the sources we reviewed, controlled studies do not show that IV NAD+ slows biological aging, prevents age-related decline or extends healthy life. Trials in heart disease give preliminary results on heart function, but they do not establish an anti-aging effect, and they cannot be transferred to healthy people or to high-dose commercial drips.

This is a conclusion about what has been demonstrated — not about what science may discover tomorrow.

The biology is real. The promised result is not demonstrated.

03 · The Safety Record

Documented harm, infusion reactions and the cost of uncertainty.

Three different things are often mixed together. We keep them apart: documented harm from a contaminated product, reactions observed during infusions, and what is still unknown.

1. A contaminated batch

FDA records a Class I recall of injectable NAD+ that began on 30 July 2025. Class I is its most serious recall category, used when a product has a reasonable probability of causing serious health consequences or death. The reason: microbial contamination of a sterile product, with elevated levels of bacterial endotoxins. (FDA Enforcement Report, recall D-0094-2026)

In January 2026, an FDA warning letter to the US facility that produced it described what happened. Three patients who received NAD+ from this batch, all from the same vial, developed low blood pressure, uncontrollable shaking, chills and body aches. All three were directed to the emergency room. An unopened vial from the same batch contained 3,360 endotoxin units per milliliter. The FDA found that the firm had not tested the finished product before release. (FDA Warning Letter, 2026)

The batch in the recall and the batch in the warning letter are the same. One story, confirmed by two regulatory documents.

This was not the first warning. In 2024, the FDA alerted compounding pharmacies that some had used NAD+ of food-grade quality to prepare IV products, and that patients had reported severe chills, shaking, vomiting and weakness, in some cases needing medical care. (FDA, 2024)

What this shows: a documented failure in how specific products were made, not a danger inherent in every infusion. But a patient cannot check product quality alone. You can ask who made the product, the batch number, where the ingredient came from, and whether it was tested for sterility and endotoxins.

In the United States, there is no FDA-approved IV NAD+ therapy for anti-aging.

2. Reactions during the infusion

In a review of commercial records, all six people who received NAD+ IV reported moderate or severe symptoms during the infusion: abdominal cramps, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, a faster heart rate, sore throat, congestion and chest pressure. The symptoms resolved after the infusion ended. ALT, AST, hsCRP, BUN/creatinine and TSH showed no significant changes. This small study cannot establish long-term safety. (Reyna et al., 2026)

In the preprint pilot, most participants who received NAD+ IV had nausea, headache, diarrhea or urgency, muscle cramps or tightness, anxiety or chest tightness; about half had a bowel movement during the infusion. White blood cells and neutrophils rose three hours later: a laboratory change, not a diagnosis. (Hawkins et al., 2024)

In the six-hour research infusion of 750 mg, no adverse reactions were reported. (Grant et al., 2019) The conference abstract reported no serious adverse effects, but it is too brief to count as evidence of safety. (Braidy, 2018)

Unpleasant symptoms during an infusion are not the same as serious adverse events. But they are part of the price.

3. What we do not know

How often reactions occur across all people who receive drips. How the dose relates to the risk. What repeated courses do over months and years. The long-term safety of repeated high-dose wellness infusions has not been adequately studied.

4. Money and time

Published prices vary widely by seller and dose. A single infusion is typically priced in the hundreds of dollars, and drips are often sold as courses of several sessions. A course can cost hundreds to thousands of dollars, without an evidence-based anti-aging dosing schedule. A commercial session lasts from about one and a half to several hours.

The bigger cost is conditional. If a drip replaces activity, blood pressure control or other measures with proven outcomes, the loss is greater than the price.

What this means

Real costs and time, for a benefit that has not been demonstrated. Short-term reactions confirmed in small studies. Documented serious reactions linked to contaminated injectable NAD+, including three emergency-room referrals. Long-term safety unknown.

The cost is real. The benefit is unproven.

Evidence quality: reactions and quality failures — confirmed by individual studies and regulatory documents. How often they occur — unknown.

04 · The Money Behind the Science

Who funded the research — and why it matters.

Who paid for the evidence?

One striking feature of the IV NAD+ evidence base is how often commercial interests appear.

Some studies were supported by companies selling NAD+ infusions. Others were funded by companies developing competing NAD-related products. Several cardiac trials received funding or study drugs from pharmaceutical manufacturers.

That does not make their results wrong.

In fact, a study funded by a company offering NAD+ infusions documented moderate or severe reactions in all six participants receiving NAD+.

Money does not decide whether a finding is true. But it gives us a reason to examine the design, analysis and interpretation more carefully — and ask whether the results have been independently replicated.

Companies offering IV NAD+

Grant et al., 2019. Funded by NAD+ Research Inc. and the Australasian Research Institute. One co-author was a director of NAD+ Research Inc. and medical director of a wellness center using IV NAD+; another received consulting fees. The study drug was supplied free of charge by Archway Apothecary.

Reyna et al., 2026. Funded by Restore Hyper Wellness, which employed all seven authors. Nicotinamide riboside was donated by Niagen Biosciences. The paper states that the funders and supplier had no role in study design, analysis or publication decisions.

Companies with competing NAD-related products

Hawkins et al., 2024 (preprint). Funded by ChromaDex, which develops and markets nicotinamide riboside products, including IV NR. Four authors were company employees, and the principal investigator's research organization was contracted by ChromaDex.

Nkrumah-Elie et al., 2026 (preprint). Funded by ChromaDex. Five authors were company employees; other investigators worked at contracted research organizations. ChromaDex disclosed patents covering injectable NR.

The drug manufacturer: cardiac trials

Pei et al., 2024. Supported by two institutional research grants and a research grant from Knature, the study drug manufacturer.

Yu et al., 2026 — heart failure. The paper reports institutional research funding, a research grant and study drug supply from Kaifeng Knature Pharmaceutical.

Yu et al., 2026 — heart attack. The published paper lists institutional research grants and declares no author conflicts of interest.

The authors of all three cardiac studies declared no personal conflicts of interest. These declarations and the disclosed funding arrangements are separate pieces of information.

Reviews and background studies

Gallagher & Emmanuel, 2026. Systematic review. The first author disclosed an interest as managing partner of a medical spa.

Janssens et al., 2022. Two co-authors were affiliated with FrieslandCampina and Danone Nutricia Research. These companies sponsored a research program that partly funded the human sample collection. The paper states they had no role in data collection, analysis or the publication decision.

Romenskaja-Avgul et al., 2026. Several authors were current or former employees of SensoLife and LifeLab1. The research used SensoLife assays; blood collection kits were provided free of charge by Bloodo. The authors reported no specific funding for the study.

McDermott et al., 2024. The oral NR trial was funded by the American Heart Association. Nicotinamide riboside was supplied by ChromaDex.

How we read this

We apply the same scrutiny to studies whose results support our verdict and those that challenge it.

A financial relationship is not evidence that a finding is false. Nor does declaring a conflict resolve every question about research independence.

We examine the methods, the reported outcomes, the funding and the possibility of independent replication.

No independently replicated clinical evidence has established that IV NAD+ slows aging in healthy adults.
