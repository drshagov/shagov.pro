VERDICT  008  Supplements

Creatine. Does it add strength after 40?

You train with weights. You take creatine. A few months later you are stronger. Creatine is one of the most studied sports supplements in the world, and it is increasingly marketed as a way to stay strong after 40. Is there evidence that it adds strength on top of what the training itself delivers?

Verdict issued and published 28 Nov 2026 Evidence last reviewed 25 Nov 2026
VerdictWhat to doThe claimTwo trialsThe age gapEvidenceSources
SHAGOV  |  VERDICT.Not proven.Issued · 28.11.2026

Studies in older adults show that creatine can add to strength gains from resistance training. For adults aged 40–54, however, direct reliable evidence is limited, and the size and practical importance of the extra benefit across adults over 40 remain insufficiently established. This does not mean creatine is ineffective.

There is not enough evidence to confirm this promise with confidence. That does not mean the method doesn’t work.

Not proven is not the same as “doesn’t work”.

Promise checked
“Creatine adds strength after 40.” In full: taking creatine monohydrate alongside regular resistance training gives a larger gain in maximal muscle strength than the same training with placebo.
Checked for
Adults aged 40 and over who do regular resistance training. Ages 40–54 and over 55 are shown separately. Pure creatine monohydrate, taken by mouth, compared with placebo under the same training programme. “Added strength” means the difference between the two groups in how much maximal strength changed, not the gain in the creatine group alone.
What this verdict does not mean

That creatine doesn’t work. Several randomised trials found an added gain in strength, mainly in older adults.

That the EU health claim is wrong. The authorised claim for adults over 55 stands, under its conditions. This review asks a wider question.

That people aged 40–54 cannot benefit. The direct evidence for them is too thin to promise it.

That creatine is unsafe. Safety is reviewed separately below. The verdict concerns added strength.

That physicians or trainers who recommend it are acting in bad faith. This review judges a claim, not people.

Not proven does not mean disproven. This verdict concerns one specific promise for one group. It is dated and will be reconsidered when stronger evidence becomes available.

Take this with you

What to do

First, plainly: this is not a recommendation to take creatine, and not a recommendation to stop.

It is a way to check a promise before you pay for it. The conditions of the studies described here are not a personal dose.

Your progress is real. It is not proof of what the supplement added.

  1. Be clear about the goal.This review is about extra maximal strength on top of resistance training. Body composition, thinking, endurance and “anti-ageing” are different promises. They were not assessed here.
  2. Compare the conditions.The most direct, regulator-recognised evidence is for pure creatine monohydrate, taken daily, with regular resistance training, in people over 55. Extending it to everyone over 40 is not established. A multi-ingredient product is not the same thing as monohydrate. (Regulation (EU) 2017/672)
  3. Don’t confuse training progress with proof that the supplement worked.A change in your own one-repetition maximum shows your progress. Without a comparison group it cannot show which part came from creatine. And no individual is owed the average gain seen in trials.
  4. Check safety and the product.If you have kidney disease or take regular medication, talk to your doctor first. Tell your doctor that you take creatine before kidney function is assessed: creatine can raise blood creatinine and change the calculated result. Check the composition and the actual dose per serving. (Tsiaras et al., 2026)
  5. Weigh the expected result against the cost.Pure monohydrate can be relatively inexpensive. A more expensive “longevity” package does not automatically come with better evidence.
Three questions before you buy

How much extra strength, shown in whom, and from what exactly?

  1. What exactly is promised?How much extra strength, in which exercise, over what time, compared with what?
  2. In whom was it shown?People of my age, training the way I train?
  3. What exactly is in the product?Monohydrate alone or a blend, and how much per serving?
01 · The Promise

One supplement. Many promises. One checked.

Creatine is a compound the body makes itself and also gets from animal foods such as meat and fish. It is stored in muscle and helps to supply energy for short, intense effort. (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements)

Sports brands promise strength, power and more repetitions. Clinicians and longevity influencers often add preserved muscle, physical independence and sharper thinking.

This review gives a verdict to one promise only: extra maximal strength on top of resistance training, in adults over 40. The other promises get no stamp here.

Resistance training makes people stronger by itself. So the question is not “did people on creatine get stronger?” It is “did they gain more than people who trained the same way on placebo?”

02 · The Regulator

The EU said yes. With an age limit.

In 2016 the European Food Safety Authority assessed a health claim for creatine. Twenty-one studies were submitted; no conclusions could be drawn from eleven of them. On the rest, EFSA accepted a cause-and-effect relationship. (EFSA, 2016)

In 2017 the claim was authorised by Regulation (EU) 2017/672: “Daily creatine consumption can enhance the effect of resistance training on muscle strength in adults over the age of 55.” (Regulation (EU) 2017/672)

The conditions of use: 3 g of creatine a day, together with resistance training performed at least three times a week for several weeks, at an intensity of at least 65–75% of one-repetition maximum, with a workload that increases over time.

The authorised claim

A narrow claim. Not a general licence.

What it isOver 55

A conditional claim for one age group, with a set daily amount and a set training regimen.

What it is notOver 40

A licence to promise strength to everyone over 40, muscle growth for all, or “longevity”.

Authorised for over 55. Marketed for over 40.

03 · Two Trials

Two trials. Opposite results.

Neither result can simply be discarded. Remove the older positive trials, and the answer for ages 40–54 does not become proven. Remove the large two-year trial, and the small positive samples cannot be treated as representative.

04 · The Age Gap

What if you’re 45?

What the evidence covers, by age

40–44No reliably extracted direct data for the main comparison.
45–54Included in mixed-age samples; no reliable separate estimate.
55–59Mixed: positive and negative trials.
60 and overPooled support for a small average added effect.

The intervals for ages 45–59 are wide. They are compatible with a benefit and with none. The grouping is by the average age of each study, not by the age of each participant, and four estimates are not necessarily four independent trials. Ages 40–54 were not analysed separately.

Results in younger athletes cannot be carried over: people under 40 are outside this question.

No advantage established. No absence of an effect established either.

05 · Why Orange

Not green. Not red.

The colour

Two ways this verdict could have been wrong.

Why not green40–54

The broad promise for everyone over 40 cannot be confirmed from evidence obtained mainly in people over 55–60. There is no agreed threshold for a “practically meaningful” gain in one-repetition maximum.

Why not redOver 55

Positive randomised trials exist. The EU recognises a narrow claim for adults over 55. The known cost and risk profile of pure monohydrate give no grounds to call its use for strength unjustified in principle.

Not proven ≠ doesn’t work.

Orange covers one promise: creatine monohydrate plus resistance training, against placebo plus the same training, in adults aged 40 and over. Not the substance as a whole, not treatment of sarcopenia, not effects on thinking.

Evidence at a glance

What was promised, and what was shown.

Implied promise
Take creatine with your training after 40, and you will gain more strength than from the training alone.
Proof needed
Randomised, placebo-controlled trials across the whole 40+ range, including ages 40–54, with the same training in both groups, measuring maximal strength and reporting the difference between the groups with its confidence interval.
Evidence
Positive trials and a small pooled effect in people aged 60 and over. An EU-authorised claim for adults over 55. A large two-year trial without a confirmed advantage. For ages 40–54: about 108 participants in studies with an average age of 45–59, and no clear result.
Trade-offs
Money; a rise in blood creatinine that can complicate the reading of kidney tests; possible stomach complaints and changes in body weight or water; variable composition of products.
The detail

Four questions. Open any one.

01

What is promised, and to whom?

More strength than training alone would give. Sold to adults over 40.

The promise, as checked: adding pure creatine monohydrate to regular resistance training produces an extra gain in maximal muscle strength, beyond the gain from the same training with placebo.

“Practically meaningful” is this review’s own criterion, not a quotation from any seller.

Who was actually studied

Mostly people over 55–60. The largest trial enrolled postmenopausal women with an average age of about 59. Direct data for ages 40–54 are scarce. (Chilibeck et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026)

What this means

The promise is made to people from 40. Most of the direct evidence comes from older age groups, while separate data for ages 40–54 remain scarce.

02

How could added strength be shown?

By the difference between two groups that train the same way. Not by progress in one group.

Getting stronger while taking creatine can mean two different things: the training worked, or the creatine added something to it. Only a comparison can tell them apart.

The comparison

Creatine plus training against placebo plus the same training, with the same rules for increasing the load.

The test

Measured one-repetition maximum, separately for the upper and the lower body. Isometric and isokinetic tests, ten-repetition tests, power, endurance, number of repetitions, muscle mass and daily function are different outcomes and are not mixed in.

Assignment, not perfect use

The primary question is what happens when people are assigned to creatine, as in real life. What happens in those who followed the regimen perfectly is a separate question and can be biased.

No universal threshold

There is no accepted medical cut-off for a meaningful gain in strength. Statistical confidence, the size of the effect and practical relevance are judged separately.

What this means

Getting stronger while taking creatine is not evidence that creatine did it.

03

What has been shown?

A small average effect in older adults. Mixed results around 55–60. Very little for 40–54.

Positive and negative trials are both kept, whatever they suggest. Each row gives the result for strength and what limits it.

Reviews

In people aged 60 and over: a small pooled effect, moderate certainty by the authors’ own rating. (Yao et al., 2026)

A review of 20 randomised trials in people aged 55 and over found a pooled gain of +2.12 kg across combined one-repetition-maximum tests; bench press and chest press taken separately were not significant, and the review’s authors rated many of the included trials as being at high risk of bias. (Sharifian et al., 2025)

A wider meta-analysis of 69 trials in adults of all ages reported its results for older adults as a subgroup, defined as over 50. In that subgroup the differences in kilograms were not significant: bench and chest press +0.85 kg (−0.64 to +2.35), leg press +1.41 kg (−6.23 to +9.05). It pooled different training programmes, and it is not an estimate for people aged 60 and over. (Kazeminasab et al., 2025)

The limits of this review. The colour is an editorial conclusion based on a register of randomised trials, published reviews, their stated limitations and an independent check of the available sources. It is not a new systematic review, and it is not presented as a completed formal GRADE assessment. Supplementary tables of two 2026 papers were not available at the time of review and are listed as limitations. (Wang et al., 2026; Chun et al., 2026) Outside the scope: treatment of sarcopenia, effects on thinking, body composition, multi-ingredient products and people under 40. If new data change the picture, the verdict will be revised with a new date.
What this means

Three statements, kept apart. An added effect in older adults: shown, small on average. The same effect for everyone over 40: not established. No effect at 40–54: not established either.

Shown in older adults. Open at 40–54.

04

What does a mistake cost?

Mostly money and a confusing blood test. Long-term data are incomplete.

For a healthy adult the known risks of pure monohydrate are modest. The gaps are in long-term data and in people with existing illness.

4.60%reported adverse events with creatine across the published literature: 685 study groups, 12,839 participants (Kreider et al., 2025)
4.21%with placebo: 652 study groups, 13,452 participants. No overall signal of harm
+0.13mg/dL: the average rise in blood creatinine across 19 randomised trials and one crossover study (95% CI 0.07–0.18) (Tsiaras et al., 2026)

Recording methods, doses and durations differed widely between studies, so rare and long-term risks are not fully excluded.

1. The blood test

No convincing overall change was found in urea or in creatinine-based estimates of kidney filtration. A second 2026 analysis of 26 studies and 1,036 participants found a similar rise in creatinine. The fall in creatinine-based estimated filtration was not matched by a comparable change in directly measured filtration, though such measurements are few. A higher creatinine is not the same as kidney damage. A reassuring result from a small direct measurement is not proof of absolute safety either. (Tsiaras et al., 2026; Meta-analysis of kidney markers, 2026)

2. Long-term safety

A review published on 17 November 2026 covered 12 trials in healthy active adults aged 18–45 and rated much of the evidence as low or very low certainty. Its authors do not consider long-term safety definitively established. How far this applies to people over 40 is limited. (Review of kidney safety, 2026)

3. Who needs individual advice

For people with chronic kidney disease, regular medication or other illnesses there are fewer data. A separate medical assessment is needed.

4. Everyday effects

Stomach complaints and changes in body weight or water are possible.

5. The product

Multi-ingredient products and uncertain composition are a separate source of risk. An expensive “longevity” package is not automatically the product that was studied.

What this means

The price of error here is modest. That is one reason the verdict is orange and not red.

SHAGOV  |  VERDICT.Not proven.Issued · 28.11.2026

Studies in older adults show that creatine can add to strength gains from resistance training. For adults aged 40–54, however, direct reliable evidence is limited, and the size and practical importance of the extra benefit across adults over 40 remain insufficiently established. This does not mean creatine is ineffective.

The promise should fit the evidence. Not the other way around.

Evidence reviewed: 25 November 2026. The verdict will be reconsidered if convincing new evidence emerges: for example, randomised placebo-controlled trials in adults aged 40–54 with the same training in both groups, reporting the difference in maximal strength with its confidence interval.

Disclosure: I have no financial ties to creatine manufacturers or sellers. This review received no commercial sponsorship.

This VERDICT evaluates the commercial promise that creatine adds strength on top of resistance training in adults over 40. It does not evaluate other uses of creatine, other supplements, or multi-ingredient products. No clinic or product is recommended.
