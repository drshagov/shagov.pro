Hyperbaric oxygen. Can it make you biologically younger?

A medical treatment for decades. Now sold as a way to turn back the clock: 60 sessions, about 90 hours in a chamber. Does the evidence support that promise?

Verdict issued and published 19 Nov 2026 Evidence last reviewed 11 Nov 2026
VerdictWhat to doKey numbersEvidenceSources
SHAGOV  |  VERDICT.Not justified.Issued · 19.11.2026

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy has not been shown to make healthy adults biologically younger. On current evidence, routine commercial courses for this purpose are not justified. This is not a finding against HBOT's established medical uses.

A medical treatment can work — without proving every benefit it is sold for.

Promise checked
A course of hyperbaric oxygen therapy can make a healthy adult biologically younger. The promise often comes with specifics: longer telomeres, fewer aging cells, a sharper brain, a fitter body.
Checked for
Healthy adults. Separately: what is known for people aged 35–50.
What this verdict does not mean

That HBOT does not work as a medical treatment. For decompression sickness, carbon monoxide poisoning and certain wounds that do not heal, it can be standard and necessary care. This verdict does not touch those uses.

That nothing changed. Some measures did change. They are shown in full below, with their size and their limits.

That physicians who offer it are acting in bad faith. This review judges a claim, not people.

Not justified does not mean proven ineffective. This verdict concerns one specific anti-aging claim. It is dated and will be reconsidered when stronger evidence becomes available.

Take this with you

What to do

First, plainly: on current evidence, a course of hyperbaric oxygen for biological rejuvenation is not justified.

What follows is for anyone who is considering a chamber anyway. It is not a guide to getting an unproven result safely, and it is not an endorsement of a course for this purpose.

The size, price and look of a chamber do not replace a check of the equipment and the staff.

  1. If you have a medical indication, decide with your doctor.A wound that does not heal or an injury after radiation therapy is a different question. There, HBOT can be ordinary, necessary treatment. This verdict does not cover it.
  2. Get checked before the first session.Ears, lungs and the list of contraindications. Over 50 or with diabetes, see an eye doctor first.
  3. Do not start a session with a cold, a blocked nose or a fever.These are relative contraindications: equalizing pressure in the ears becomes harder.
  4. Count the sessions.Temporary nearsightedness grows with the number of sessions, and in studies it was smaller with a mask than with a hood. That is a difference in risk, not a safe setup. For long packages and repeated courses, no safe number of sessions has been established.
  5. Ask about the people and the room.Trained staff, someone watching you for the whole session, fire-safety rules followed. (FDA, 2025)
One question before you pay

What exactly am I paying for, and how will you show that I received the promised result?

  1. What pressure, and what oxygen, will I actually breathe?Ask for the pressure in absolute atmospheres (ATA), the oxygen concentration you actually inhale, and whether it comes through a mask, a hood or the chamber air. The research used 2.0 ATA and 100% oxygen through a mask. Some chambers sold under the same name work at much lower pressure, on ordinary air.
  2. How many sessions, and how long is each one?Ask about minutes at pressure, minutes on oxygen and air breaks. Sixty minutes in a chamber are not necessarily sixty minutes of breathing pure oxygen. The studies behind the promise used 40 to 60 sessions of 60 to 90 minutes; most offers sell fewer and shorter.
  3. What will be measured, and when?Agree on it before you start. A measurement soon after the course shows whether something changed; another several months later shows whether it lasted. A before-and-after change does not prove the course caused it. And neither a telomere test nor a higher VO₂max shows that you became biologically younger.
01 · The Promise

A medical treatment. Now sold as a youth course.

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy means breathing oxygen at higher than normal air pressure, inside a sealed chamber.

Medicine has used it for decades: for divers with decompression sickness, for carbon monoxide poisoning, for certain wounds that do not heal. Aging is not among the conditions for which the FDA has cleared hyperbaric chambers. (FDA)

Since 2020, a small set of human studies has reported changes after a full course: in telomere measurements in blood cells, in cognitive test scores, in aerobic fitness. The market turned those findings into a promise: become biologically younger.

What a full course costs

A course "as in the research" is 60 sessions of 90 minutes, five days a week, for 12 weeks. That is about 90 hours in a chamber, before travel and preparation.

Published prices for chamber-only courses run to several thousand dollars. One Dubai hospital lists 40 sessions at 32,000 AED, about 8,700 US dollars. Programs that bundle the chamber with training and testing reach about 48,600 US dollars; that is the price of a package, not of the chamber alone. Prices change; this is an illustration, not a market index.

02 · The Chain

Four steps of the sales story. Where it breaks.

03 · Same Word

One name. Different treatments.

The studies behind the promise used one specific regimen. What is sold under the same name often differs in every parameter that matters.

The home chamber

At home, a different exposure.

The research protocol2.0 ATA

100% oxygen through a mask. 60 sessions of 90 minutes. A hard chamber in a medical center.

A cleared soft chamber1.2–1.3 ATA

Ordinary air. One example of an FDA-cleared portable chamber is cleared for acute mountain sickness, not anti-aging. (FDA 510(k) K072757) The FDA has not cleared these zippered chambers for use with oxygen tanks or concentrators. (FDA) No study of telomeres or rejuvenation used a regimen like this.

One documented example, not a claim about every home chamber. Other units add oxygen concentrators or use other settings: ask what you actually inhale. A device clearance for one use is not proof that HBOT reverses aging.

Same name ≠ same treatment.

The strongest version of the claim

We judge the promise in its strongest form: a clinic that reproduces the research protocol exactly. Even then, it reproduces the exposure, not proof of rejuvenation. The questions about control groups, the meaning of the markers and the lasting effect remain.

Anything that differs from the studied regimen has less evidence, not more. A bigger chamber, a higher pressure or a longer course does not change that. Medicine uses pressures up to 3.0 atmospheres for specific conditions, but higher is not better: the risk profile changes.

Matching the protocol settles the question of comparability. It does not settle the question of proof.

04 · Why Now

More science. A clearer limit.

Five to ten years ago, rejuvenation through HBOT was an intriguing hypothesis with almost no human data. Today there is more evidence. It shows both what happens and where the evidence stops.

  • The telomere signal has been seen twice, in two countries. Both studies had no control group.
  • Functional improvements were observed, one to two weeks after the course. Where there was no sham control, how much of them HBOT caused is not established.
  • The only cognitive trial in older adults with a sham control and 12 months of follow-up found no benefit.
  • In the athletes' trial, the registry planned a check at four months. That result has not been published, and we could not find it.
  • No study measured biological age or health outcomes.

A research question can stay scientifically interesting while buying a costly course for the promised result becomes unjustified. The color follows the specific claim, the people, the evidence and the price of error, not the number of publications, and not whether the technology has legitimate uses elsewhere.

More research does not automatically mean more evidence for the promise.

The regulator's view

In Dubai, HBOT is medicine.

As a treatmentYes

Dubai's health authority sets standards for hyperbaric oxygen services, for recognised indications. (DHA, 2022)

To make you youngerNo

The same standard says HBOT should not be used for unproven purposes, and names anti-aging among them. Uses outside recognised indications belong in approved clinical studies. (DHA, 2022) In Abu Dhabi, the standard for longevity clinics lists telomere length testing as not recommended outside a research protocol. (DoH Abu Dhabi, 2024)

Medical use ≠ anti-aging proof.

The verdict is not borrowed from a regulator. It follows from the human evidence, the outcome that is promised and the price of error.

Evidence at a glance

What was promised, and what was shown.

Implied promise
A course of hyperbaric oxygen makes a healthy adult biologically younger: longer telomeres, fewer aging cells, a sharper brain, a fitter body.
Proof needed
Sham-controlled trials in healthy adults, with validated measures linked to health or with health outcomes themselves, follow-up of six to twelve months or longer, and independent replication. A change in one biomarker would not be enough.
Evidence
A telomere signal in blood cells rose in two uncontrolled studies. Short-term gains in cognitive scores and aerobic fitness, measured one to two weeks after the course; sham-controlled only in trained athletes aged 40–50. Biological age and health outcomes were not measured. The one sham-controlled trial with 12 months of follow-up found no cognitive benefit, in a different population.
Trade-offs
Common, mostly mild ear problems and temporary nearsightedness. Rare serious events. About 90 hours in a chamber. Thousands of dollars for a full course.
The detail

Four questions. Open any one.

01

What is promised, and to whom?

Become biologically younger. Sold to healthy adults, often with telomeres as the proof.

The promise, as checked: a course of hyperbaric oxygen therapy makes a healthy adult biologically younger. It is offered to healthy adults, including people aged 35 to 50.

How the promise sounds

Phrases on company pages include "reverse the biology of aging", "lengthens telomeres" and "regenerate on a cellular level". In our sample of 18 offers, 15 openly promised rejuvenation, anti-aging, or slowing or reversing aging; 8 named telomeres as the argument. Offering better function alone, without any word about rejuvenation, was the exception.

What is actually sold

Few offers state a regimen close to the studied one. Most sell fewer sessions, shorter sessions, a lower or unstated pressure, or oxygen from a concentrator, where the figure on the device is not the oxygen a person actually inhales. In 9 of the 18 offers, the pressure is not stated at all. Soft chambers at 1.3–1.5 atmospheres use the words "anti-aging" and "lengthens telomeres", although no telomere study used such a regimen. Some programs sell 10–20 sessions while pointing to a 60-session study. In bundled programs, the chamber comes with training and nutrition, so its own effect cannot be separated.

Who was actually studied

Older adultsFive publications, one cohortPeople aged 64 and over, living independently, without pathological cognitive decline. Not "healthy" in the strict sense: some had diabetes, high blood pressure or coronary disease. (Hadanny et al., 2020)The five papers report different measures from one research program and one registered cohort. They are not five independent confirmations.
Trained athletesOne trialAmateur athletes aged 40–50, training four or more times a week. (Hadanny et al., 2022)The only sham-controlled result, and the only one in the 35–50 age range.
A mixed cohortOne studyAdults aged 20–79: practically healthy people and patients treated for medical indications. (Chu et al., 2026)No separate result for ages 35–50.
What this means

The promise is made to healthy adults of any age. The positive studies were done in narrower groups: older adults from one cohort, trained athletes, and a mixed cohort dominated by patients. The group that is promised and the groups that were studied do not match.

02

How can the result be checked?

Only with validated measures or health outcomes, months after the course. No study has done that.

To say that a person became biologically younger, you would need a recognised measure of biological aging or health outcomes, a sham control, a measurement months after the course, and independent replication.

Four levels. What was studied

1. BiomarkersTelomeres, aging cells, epigenetic clocksTelomere signal in blood cells in two studies; the share of aging T cells; skin samples from 13 men; gene activity in 27 people. Telomerase was not measured in any study. A study of epigenetic clocks in people aged 40–70 was registered; we found no results. The signal shifted in the "younger" direction. Both telomere studies had no control group.
2. Short-term functionWeeks after the courseCognitive tests and VO₂max, one to two weeks after the course, in three trials from one research group; and one sham-controlled trial in older adults with diabetes. Improvement in the first three. No benefit in the sham-controlled trial.
3. Lasting change6–12 months, with a controlIn the positive studies, no follow-up beyond two weeks. The athletes' registry planned a check at four months; no result published. The 12-month sham-controlled trial found no lasting difference. Not studied in healthy adults.
4. Health outcomesDisease, independence, years of lifeNo study. Not studied.

When to check

Short-term measurements can show whether a functional change occurred. Measurements months later can test whether it persists. Neither a before-and-after comparison nor a telomere test alone can establish biological rejuvenation.

Telomere length is not a ruler for age

In blood, telomere length depends on which cells are circulating at the moment and on measurement error. Apparent lengthening in repeated measurements is often explained by the error of the method, especially over short periods. (Steenstrup et al., 2013) Not everyone agrees that it is only error; true lengthening cannot be fully excluded. (Bateson & Nettle, 2017)

Lengthening is not always good news. In the NASA twin study, an astronaut's telomeres lengthened during a year in space and shortened rapidly after his return; the researchers link this to chronic oxidative stress. (Garrett-Bakelman et al., 2019) And in a large genetic analysis, inherited longer telomeres were linked to a higher risk of several cancers and a lower risk of some other diseases, including coronary heart disease. (Haycock et al., 2017)

Abu Dhabi's standard for longevity clinics lists telomere length testing as not recommended outside a research protocol. (DoH Abu Dhabi, 2024)

Can the buyer check?

In our sample, 13 of 18 offers gave no way to check the result. One offered telomere length before and after — a measure that does not confirm rejuvenation. Bundled programs test function before and after: that can be checked, but without a control group, and it is not biological age.

What this means

The promise is about biological age. No study measured it, or any health outcome. A person who is promised to become younger usually gets no way to check it; where a check is offered, the measure does not answer the question.

03

What has been shown in people?

Short-term, measurable changes in narrow groups. Biological rejuvenation was never measured.

The positive data on people without disease come from one research program: five publications from one cohort of older adults, plus one trial in athletes by the same group. A separate cohort in China is linked to a chamber manufacturer. Commercial ties are a reason to ask for independent replication, not a reason to dismiss the results.

Telomeres · no control group

Hachmo et al., 202025 analysed of 35 enrolled · age 64+60 sessions at 2.0 atmospheres. Relative telomere length in four types of blood immune cells rose by about 20–38% on average; across all time points, the overall test was significant only for B cells. The share of aging (CD28-negative) helper T cells fell from 10.3% to 6.2%. Last measurement: up to two weeks after the course. (Hachmo et al., 2020)No control group. The spread between people was larger than the average effect. Several inconsistencies between text and tables. The authors name the small sample, the missing control and the unknown duration as limits.
Chu et al., 202697 analysed · age 20–79, mostly patients30 sessions at 2.5 atmospheres. The telomere signal in pooled blood cells rose by 13.9%, 20.3% and 29.0% after 10, 20 and 30 sessions. Age did not change the trajectory; there is no separate result for ages 35–50. (Chu et al., 2026)No control group. The method is sensitive to shifts in the mix of blood cells, which the authors acknowledge. Funded by a chamber manufacturer.

Telomerase, the enzyme that can lengthen telomeres, was not measured in any human study of HBOT. For a rise in blood telomere length to count as rejuvenation, three things would need to be shown: that telomeres lengthened in the cells themselves, rather than the mix of cells changing; that the effect lasts; and that longer blood telomeres mean better health. None has been shown.

The two studies used different assays, cell types and protocols, so their percentages cannot be pooled as one result. The fall in CD28-negative T cells is an interesting immune observation, not proof that aging cells were cleared throughout the body.

What this means

A measurable shift in a blood signal during and right after a course. It deserves a controlled study. It is not a measure of a younger body.

Reliability of the effect: low. Strength for the promise: very weak.

The brain · no sham control

Hadanny et al., 202061 analysed · age 64+60 sessions against no intervention. The global cognitive score rose by 5.2 points, against 0.8 in controls: a net difference of about 4.4 points on a scale where 100 is the population average and 15 is one standard deviation. Attention and processing speed improved; the memory gain did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. Tested one to two weeks after the course. (Hadanny et al., 2020)The control group received nothing, and participants knew their group, which matters for tests sensitive to expectation and motivation. The groups differed at baseline.

Endurance in older adults · no sham control

Hadanny et al., 202463 · the same cohortVO₂max per kilogram rose by 1.91 ml/kg/min, about 10%, while it fell by 0.80 in controls. In absolute terms, +111 ml/min against −67: a net difference of about 178 ml/min. (Hadanny et al., 2024)The same limits. Part of the net effect comes from the decline in the control group. The groups differed at baseline.

The strongest result · trained athletes 40–50, sham-controlled

Hadanny et al., 202231 analysed of 37 · age 40–5040 sessions at 2.0 atmospheres, 100% oxygen, 60 minutes, over about eight weeks, against a sham. VO₂max rose from about 2,834 to 2,956 ml/min (+122, or +4.3% from the group's own baseline) and fell from about 2,673 to 2,631 ml/min with the sham: a net difference of about 164 ml/min, p = 0.010. The 4.3% is a change within the HBOT group, not the difference against the sham. Power output rose by 4.9%. Mitochondrial measures in muscle biopsies improved in 10–12 people per group. (Hadanny et al., 2022)Double-blind, but blinding was checked only after the first session. The groups differed at baseline: VO₂max per kilogram 42.0 against 36.1, and only runners and triathletes in the HBOT group. Six people left after randomisation. Tested one to two weeks after the course. The registry planned a check at four months; that result has not been published. An unreported result is a gap in knowledge, not proof of selective publication. (NCT03524989)

This is the best-designed result in the program. It answers a question about athletic performance in trained people. Age, aging and health were not measured.

The counterweight · sham-controlled, 12 months

Benari et al., 2025155 · age 65+, type 2 diabetes, mild cognitive impairmentDouble-blind, against a sham. HBOT did not improve cognition or brain measures compared with the sham, at 3, 6 or 12 months. At 3 months, the sham group scored better on the global score and executive function. Everyone improved from baseline, including the sham group. (Benari et al., 2025)Not healthy adults. A brief, abstract-format publication. Co-authors from the same research group. More serious adverse events were reported in the HBOT group (25 against 8); whether these are events or people, and what they were, is not disclosed.

Everyone improved, including the sham group. That is why trials without a sham control can overstate an effect.

It is a useful counterweight, not a direct refutation of every positive result: the people were different, and so was the question.

Skin and genes

In 13 men aged about 68, skin biopsies after the course showed denser collagen, more blood vessels and fewer aging cells, compared with their own control period; skin appearance was not assessed. (Hachmo et al., 2021) In 27 people without a control group, the activity of 1,912 genes in blood changed. (Hadanny et al., 2021) These are descriptions of change, not evidence of benefit.

How big are these effects?

In older adults, the chamber added 1.91 ml/kg/min of VO₂max. Aerobic training in sedentary older adults adds 3.78 ml/kg/min on average, across 41 controlled trials; in people aged 70 and over, about 1.56. (Huang et al., 2005; Huang et al., 2016; Bouaziz et al., 2018) No one calls a training-induced rise in VO₂max a reversal of biological age: it is an adaptation that fades when the training stops.

Higher fitness is linked to lower mortality: each 1 MET, or 3.5 ml/kg/min, is associated with 13% lower all-cause mortality. (Kodama et al., 2009) That is an observational link between fitness level and survival. Whether a VO₂max gain from a chamber course lowers mortality or slows aging has not been tested.

Four statements, kept apart

  1. Function improved. Shown: in the short term, in narrow groups.
  2. The improvement is statistically significant. Shown for the main measures; some secondary results without correction for multiple comparisons.
  3. The improvement lasts. Not tested. The registered four-month check in athletes has not been published.
  4. The person became biologically younger. Not measured, and it does not follow from the first three.

The reverse also holds: the absence of proven rejuvenation does not mean the absence of a functional effect.

What is known for ages 35–50

Data for people under 50 exist, but they answer narrower questions: athletic endurance in trained people aged 40–50, and blood markers in a mixed cohort where age did not change the result. Ordinary healthy people aged 35–50 who are not athletes were not studied. There are no data on biological rejuvenation at this age.

A separate question

Does a course of HBOT improve aerobic endurance in trained people aged 40–50? That is a different question, and research has already produced interesting results on it. It deserves its own scientific review. A topic for a future SHAGOV | VERDICT.

A 2026 systematic review of HBOT for physical and cognitive performance in older adults found 7 studies with 502 participants aged 51–85, including clinical groups. Its authors call the positive data preliminary: few studies, different protocols, moderate quality, follow-up of about 12 weeks. (Mohammadi et al., 2026)

The limits of this review. This is a targeted audit of the sources, read in full text where available, not an exhaustive systematic review of every database. Our ratings of evidence quality are editorial judgments, not a completed formal grading. Outside the scope: recognised medical indications, HBOT for brain injury or PTSD, and the efficacy of soft chambers for other conditions.
What this means

A full course in the research regimen is followed by short-term, measurable changes: in attention and processing speed in older adults, with the caveat of no sham control; and in aerobic fitness, by about 10% in older adults and about 4% in trained people aged 40–50. These are observed physiological changes, comparable in size to the effects of training or smaller. Only in the athletes' trial was there a sham control; elsewhere, how much HBOT itself caused is not fully established.

None of the studies measured whether a healthy person became biologically younger.

Measured changes. An unmeasured promise.

Evidence quality: low for markers; moderate for short-term function; absent for rejuvenation.

04

What does a mistake cost?

Thousands of dollars, about 90 hours, common mild side effects and rare serious ones — for a result that was not measured.

For recognised indications, with proper equipment and supervision, HBOT is routine clinical care. The question here is narrower: is it justified to put a healthy person through 40 to 60 sessions for a promise of rejuvenation?

Two cautions. Almost all risk data come from patients, not healthy people, so the percentages cannot be transferred directly in either direction. And a risk per session is not a risk per course.

30% vs 10%any adverse event, HBOT against control, in 24 randomized trials
15%ear complications across 54 studies and 18,284 patients
70% → 91%temporary nearsightedness after 18 and after 30 sessions, single-person chambers

1. Ears: the most common problem

A meta-analysis of 24 randomized trials with 1,497 participants found adverse events in 30.1% with HBOT against 10.4% in controls. Ear problems were the most frequent; eye problems (nearsightedness, changes in visual acuity) came next. Seizures, sinus pain, claustrophobia and headache showed no significant difference, with wide intervals. Trials at 2.0 atmospheres or more showed a larger difference against their controls than trials below 2.0: a comparison within each subgroup, not proof that pressure multiplies the risk, and the absence of a difference below 2.0 does not prove safety. Participants were patients aged 5 to 70; only one trial involved healthy older adults. (Zhang et al., 2023)

Across 54 studies and 18,284 patients, ear complications occurred in 15%. Among middle-ear barotraumas, 42.8% were mild and 6.4% severe. The risk was higher in older people, women, and people who find it hard to equalize pressure. (Voigt et al., 2025)

2. Eyes: the risk grows with the number of sessions

A temporary shift toward nearsightedness is a common side effect, caused by changes in the lens. In a systematic review of 8 studies and 263 patients, it occurred in 70% after 18 sessions and 91% after 30 in single-person chambers. The shift grew with the number of sessions and was smallest with a mask in a multi-person chamber, larger in single-person chambers, and largest with a hood. (Evanger et al., 2026) In a randomized trial of 120 patients, the shift was larger with a hood than with a mask, and recovery with a hood may be slower and less complete. (Bennett et al., 2019)

In the older-adult program behind the promise, changes in visual acuity were reported in 62% of the HBOT group against 37% of controls. (Hadanny et al., 2020)

Cataract. With a usual series of 20–50 sessions, a new cataract is rare in the experience of large centers. Beyond about 100 sessions, the risk of irreversible changes and new cataract rises. (UHMS) In an older study of 25 patients after 150 or more sessions, 7 of the 15 who started with a clear lens developed a nuclear cataract with reduced vision. (Palmquist et al., 1984) No safe number of sessions has been established.

The research protocol used a mask in a multi-person chamber, the gentlest setup for the eyes among those studied. But 60 sessions is more than the 40 at the upper end of the eye review. Some offers sell packages of up to 100 sessions and maintenance courses; repeated courses add to the load on the lens.

3. Rare but serious

Oxygen seizures. In a naval hyperbaric center, 188,335 sessions in 8,537 patients at 2.5 atmospheres: on average 2.3 seizures per 10,000 sessions. After five-minute air breaks were introduced, 1.2 against 3.9 per 10,000. No lasting harm is described. The pressure was higher than in the research protocol, and the rate has not been measured in healthy people. (Costa et al., 2019)

Lung barotrauma. Rare and potentially serious; the risk is higher with lung diseases that trap air or a past pneumothorax. Fire. Extremely rare, but catastrophic in an oxygen-rich environment.

In August 2025, the FDA wrote to health care providers about reports of serious injuries and deaths with hyperbaric chambers, including recent fires. It called for following the manufacturer's instructions, fire safety and grounding, trained staff, watching the patient for the whole session, maintenance, and cotton clothing. The FDA noted that serious events are rare. (FDA, 2025)

4. Who should not start without a doctor

The only absolute contraindication is an untreated pneumothorax. Relative ones need a doctor's assessment: a past pneumothorax, lung bullae or COPD; insulin-treated diabetes, because of the risk of low blood sugar; uncontrolled high blood pressure; fever, sinusitis or a cold; a history of seizures; pregnancy; implanted devices; some medicines; severe claustrophobia; recent eye surgery. (StatPearls)

5. Money and time

About 90 hours in a chamber over 12 weeks for a course as in the research, before travel, preparation and tests. Chamber-only courses cost several thousand dollars; bundled programs, tens of thousands.

What this means

The main cost here is not a complication. It is money and time spent on a result that cannot be established.

Declining is not declared the guaranteed right choice. Short-term functional effects have been studied; long-term clinical benefit remains a research question. The absence of an established benefit is not proof that it is impossible.

The cost is real. The promised result is unmeasured.

Evidence quality: common side effects — confirmed by meta-analyses in patients. Risk for healthy people over 60 sessions and repeated courses — unknown.

Who paid for the evidence?

Funders and author ties of the key studies, named on both sides.

The positive human data come mostly from one research program whose authors include employees and shareholders of a company that develops commercial HBOT programs. A separate cohort was funded by a chamber manufacturer. That does not make the results wrong. It is a reason to look closely at the design and to ask for independent replication. We name funders for the studies behind this verdict, whether they support it or not.

The older-adult and athlete program

Hachmo et al., 2020 (telomeres). Funded by a Sagol network grant. Three authors are employees of AVIV Scientific; the head of the group is a shareholder.

Hadanny et al., 2020 (cognition). Funded by a Sagol network grant. Six authors are employees of AVIV Scientific; the head of the group is a shareholder.

Hachmo et al., 2021 (skin). Funded by a Sagol network grant. Several authors are employees of AVIV Scientific; the head of the group is a shareholder.

Hadanny et al., 2022 (athletes) and Hadanny et al., 2024 (endurance). Funded by the Shamir Medical Center research fund. Four authors are employees of AVIV Scientific; the head of the group is a shareholder and co-founder.

The negative trial

Benari et al., 2025. Funding is not stated in the publication. Co-authors include leaders of the same research program. It is not a fully independent test.

The chamber manufacturer

Chu et al., 2026. Funded by Canta Medical Tech under an agreement with the university. One author is the company's president.

How we read this

We apply the same scrutiny to studies whose results support our verdict and those that challenge it. A financial relationship is not evidence that a finding is false. Nor does declaring a conflict resolve every question about independence. Publications by authors with shared commercial ties and a shared cohort do not count as independent confirmations of each other.

No independently replicated evidence has established that HBOT makes healthy adults biologically younger.

SHAGOV  |  VERDICT.Not justified.Issued · 19.11.2026

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy has not been shown to make healthy adults biologically younger. On current evidence, routine commercial courses for this purpose are not justified. This is not a finding against HBOT's established medical uses.

Evidence reviewed: 11 November 2026. The verdict will be reconsidered if convincing new evidence emerges.

Disclosure: in 2018–2020, I used hyperbaric oxygen therapy in clinical practice for anti-aging purposes and to improve physical performance, including endurance.

This VERDICT evaluates HBOT as a way to become biologically younger in healthy adults. It does not evaluate recognised medical indications. No clinic, chamber or program is named or recommended here.

Update log. 19 Nov 2026: first publication. Verdict: NOT JUSTIFIED, issued 19 Nov 2026. Evidence reviewed: 11 Nov 2026. This verdict will be reconsidered when stronger evidence becomes available; updates will be dated here and earlier versions kept.
