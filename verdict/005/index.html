VERDICT  005  Hormones

Testosterone. Can testosterone therapy improve low sexual desire in men with confirmed hypogonadism?

Testosterone is sold for energy, drive, focus and youth. One promise has strong placebo-controlled trials behind it, and in 2026 the FDA invited manufacturers to make the case for it. What does the evidence show, and for whom?

Verdict issued and published 21 Nov 2026 Evidence last reviewed 12 Nov 2026
VerdictWhat to doKey numbersEvidenceSources
SHAGOV  |  VERDICT.Proven.Issued · 21.11.2026Only if

Testosterone therapy has been shown to improve low sexual desire in men with confirmed hypogonadism. On average the added benefit is small, and not every man notices an improvement. This is not a finding that testosterone treats aging, fatigue or low motivation.

A proven treatment is not a universal recommendation.

Only if
  1. Diagnosis confirmed, causes of low testosterone assessed.
  2. Fertility plans, contraindications and risks reviewed before treatment.
  3. Treatment is monitored, and the result is judged by change in sexual desire, not just blood testosterone.

The red clip marks a method that works, where ignoring the conditions can cause clinically significant harm.

Age limit: the strongest evidence comes from middle-aged and older men. Direct trial evidence for ages 35–44 is much more limited.

What this verdict covers, and what it does not
Promise checked
In men with confirmed hypogonadism, testosterone therapy can improve low sexual desire compared with placebo.
Checked for
Men with low sexual desire and clinically and biochemically confirmed hypogonadism. Separately: what is known for men aged 35–50.
What this verdict does not mean

That testosterone is a general treatment for aging, fatigue, low motivation or reduced cognitive performance. Those are different promises. They need their own review.

That every man with low desire needs testosterone. Low desire has many causes, and many men with low desire have normal testosterone.

That treatment is free of risk. Fertility, blood counts, heart rhythm and clotting are part of the decision.

Proven does not mean right for everyone. This verdict concerns one specific use. It is dated and will be reconsidered when new evidence changes the picture.

Take this with you

What to do

In short: if your sex drive has dropped, don't start with testosterone. Start with a diagnosis.

A testosterone number is a marker. The result is a change in desire.

  1. Confirm the diagnosis first.At least two early-morning fasting blood tests, an assessment of symptoms, and a search for the cause. One low number is not a diagnosis.
  2. Know when it is not considered.If your testosterone is normal. If your goal is energy, motivation, focus or "rejuvenation". If you plan to have a child soon.
  3. Cause first.If excess weight is the only identified cause, weight loss is the first step of treatment. If medications lower your testosterone (opioids, glucocorticoids), discuss them with your doctor first.
  4. Reduce the risk.Testosterone and hematocrit at 3, 6 and 12 months, then yearly. Hematocrit above 54% is a reason for your doctor to adjust or stop treatment. Prostate (PSA) by age and risk, blood pressure regularly, blood clots in you or your family discussed before starting. Do not change the dose yourself.
  5. Choose approved products.They deliver the same molecule the body makes, with known composition and studied dosing. "Bioidentical" does not mean "better", and a "physiological dose" is not the same as "safe".
  6. Check the result, not the lab number.Before starting, write down where your sex drive is now: a short scale or a one-week diary. Changes usually appear within 3–6 weeks; review the result with your doctor at about 8 weeks. Safety tests follow their own schedule. Decide whether to continue no later than 12 months: no improvement means treatment is reconsidered or stopped.
Five questions before you start

How was this diagnosed, and how will we know it is working?

  1. How was the diagnosis confirmed?How many tests, at what time of day, fasting or not, and by what method.
  2. What cause was found?And what has been done about the causes that can be fixed: weight, sleep, medications, other hormones.
  3. How will treatment affect my ability to have children?Testosterone suppresses sperm production. Full recovery after years of use is not guaranteed.
  4. Which tests before starting, and how often will I be monitored?Hematocrit, PSA by age and risk, blood pressure.
  5. When will we know it is working, and when do we stop if it is not?Agree on the measure and the date before the first dose.
01 · Why This Question

Testosterone is sold for almost everything. We started where its evidence is strongest.

Energy, strength, motivation, focus, youth. The market attaches all of them to one hormone. We checked the promise where testosterone has the most direct human evidence, so the line between data and marketing would be easy to see.

In a large European study, researchers asked which of 32 symptoms often blamed on "andropause" actually track with low testosterone. Nine did. Only three formed a pattern that defines the syndrome, and all three were sexual. This is an observational study: it explains why we began here. It does not prove that treatment helps. (Wu et al., 2010)

Desire is also where the strongest trials are. It has been compared with placebo in randomized, double-blind studies many times. And in April 2026, the FDA invited makers of approved testosterone products to discuss a possible new indication: low libido in men with low testosterone and no identifiable cause. (FDA, 2026) That is context, not evidence and not an approval.

02 · The Numbers

Small on average. Noticeable for some.

Two different measures, which should not be mixed: the average difference from placebo, and the share of men who felt a meaningful change.

The average difference is small. In the TTrials, desire improved by 2.93 points more than with placebo on a validated scale. In TRAVERSE, by about 3–4 points on a 0–100 scale, where a meaningful change for one person is about 8 points. (Cunningham et al., 2016; Pencina et al., 2024; Gelhorn et al., 2016) A meta-analysis commissioned by the Endocrine Society rated the effect on desire as small but significant. (Ponce et al., 2018)

But for a share of men, the change is noticeable. In a validation sample of the TTrials, researchers counted how many men reached a change they themselves rated as meaningful.

Desire usually starts to change about three weeks into treatment and levels off by about six weeks; changes in erections can take up to six months. (Saad et al., 2011) In TRAVERSE, the improvement in desire held for up to 24 months. (Pencina et al., 2024)

03 · The Age Question

But what if you are 42?

The trials behind this verdict studied middle-aged and older men. That matters for anyone younger.

35–44Least evidenceNot represented in TRAVERSE or the TTrials. Under 3% of men in the largest pooled analysis were younger than 40. One short trial included men aged 19–92 but gave no separate result. No trial-measured size of the effect on desire at this age.
45–50The lower edgeThe youngest end of TRAVERSE. Not analysed separately. Included in the studied group, but not represented by it.
50–64The main baseAbout half of TRAVERSE; the T4DM trial; a 12-week trial with a mean age of 55. Many of these men had obesity, metabolic disease or high cardiovascular risk. Solid evidence, with that caveat.
65+The densest dataThe TTrials and the older half of TRAVERSE. The 36% versus 14% comes from here.

Two statements need to stay separate. Treating hypogonadism caused by disease of the testes, the pituitary or the hypothalamus is established practice at any age. But the size of the effect on desire has been measured in large trials only in middle-aged and older men. In one trial the effect was larger in older men; in another, it looked larger at 65+ but the difference was not significant. (Wittert et al., 2025; Pencina et al., 2024) No significant difference is not proof that the effect is the same at every age.

A result in men aged 65+ is not a personal forecast for a 42-year-old.

04 · The Price of Error

Planning to have children? This changes the decision.

Testosterone taken from outside suppresses the signals the testes need to make sperm. For men aged 35–50, this is the first risk to weigh.

In a WHO study of testosterone as male contraception, 157 of 271 healthy men stopped producing sperm. That is a contraceptive regimen, not usual replacement therapy, but the mechanism is the same. (WHO Task Force, 1990) After short-term use in such studies, sperm production returned in 67% of men by 6 months and 90% by 12 months. (Liu et al., 2006) After years of therapy, recovery is slower in older men and after longer use, and full recovery is not guaranteed. (Kohn et al., 2017) European urology guidelines strongly advise against testosterone for men who want to become fathers. (EAU, 2026)

Other known effects: a rise in red blood cells is about eight times more likely than with placebo (Ponce et al., 2018); in 2025 the FDA added a warning about increased blood pressure to all testosterone products (FDA, 2025).

TRAVERSE kept testosterone in the normal range and still saw these signals. A physiological dose lowers some risks. It does not make treatment risk-free.

05 · The Regulator

Proven, recommended, approved. Three different things.

Where things stand

Science, guidelines and labels move at different speeds.

Proven in trialsYes

Placebo-controlled trials show improved desire in men with confirmed hypogonadism. This verdict.

Recommended by expertsYes

The International Consultation on Sexual Medicine recommends testosterone to improve libido in men with hypogonadism: strong recommendation, high-quality evidence. (Rastrelli et al., ICSM 2024)

Approved for low libidoNot yet

In the US, testosterone is approved for hypogonadism with a known cause. In April 2026 the FDA invited applications for low libido without an identifiable cause. That is an invitation, not an approval. (FDA, 2026)

Invitation ≠ approval.

Timeline

  • February 2025. Class-wide label changes: the boxed warning on cardiovascular risk removed, TRAVERSE results and a blood pressure warning added; the limitation on age-related hypogonadism kept. (FDA, 2025)
  • December 2025. FDA expert panel on testosterone therapy.
  • 16 April 2026. FDA invites holders of approved products to discuss a new indication: low libido in men with idiopathic hypogonadism. (FDA, 2026)
  • 18 June 2026. HHS and FDA request label updates, including removal of the age-related limitation. These take effect only after manufacturers submit and the FDA approves the changes. (HHS, 2026)

Checked on 12 November 2026: the Testim (July 2025) and AndroGel 1.62% (October 2025) labels still state that safety and efficacy in age-related hypogonadism have not been established. The verdict is not borrowed from a regulator. It follows from the human evidence, the outcome that is promised and the price of error.

Evidence at a glance

What was promised, and what was shown.

Implied promise
In men with confirmed hypogonadism, testosterone therapy can improve low sexual desire compared with placebo.
Proof needed
Randomized, placebo-controlled trials in men with confirmed hypogonadism and low desire, with validated measures of desire, follow-up of at least a year, and an estimate of how many men feel a meaningful change.
Evidence
Several placebo-controlled trials, all in the same direction, and meta-analyses. Small average effect; a meaningful improvement in about 36% versus 14% at 12 months in men aged 65+. Little direct data for ages 35–44.
Trade-offs
Suppressed sperm production. More red blood cells. In the largest trial, more atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, kidney injury and fractures. Long-term safety not fully established.
The detail

Four questions. Open any one.

01

What is promised, and to whom?

Better sexual desire, for men whose low testosterone is confirmed. Not energy, not youth.

The promise, as checked: in men with confirmed hypogonadism, testosterone therapy can improve low sexual desire compared with placebo.

Who is in the checked group

Men with low sexual desire as a complaint, with other compatible symptoms, and with hypogonadism confirmed by at least two early-morning fasting tests using a standardized method. The usual threshold is about 300 ng/dL (about 12 nmol/L in European guidance); borderline results need free testosterone and SHBG.

Who is not

Men with normal testosterone. Men without symptoms whose low number was found "just in case". Men whose goal is energy, motivation, focus, strength or rejuvenation. Those are different promises.

Why "hypogonadism", not "low testosterone"

Hypogonadism is a clinical syndrome, not a single lab value. The Endocrine Society warns that terms such as "age-related", "late-onset" and "functional" hypogonadism blur the line between disease and normal aging. (Endocrine Society, 2026)

The molecule and the market

Approved products, gels and injectable esters, deliver the same molecule the body makes. Advantages of custom-compounded "bioidentical" preparations have not been shown, and their quality depends on how they are made. (FDA, 2019) Intramuscular forms cost less with a comparable effect. (ACP, 2020)

What this means

The verdict is narrow on purpose: one outcome, one confirmed group. Selling testosterone as a general booster for men with normal levels is outside it.

02

How can the result be checked?

By a change in desire, against your own baseline. Not by a higher number on a lab report.

Desire, sexual activity and erections are three different outcomes. Trials often group them as "sexual function"; here they are kept apart.

How to measure

Before starting, record a baseline: a short validated scale or a one-week diary. Agree on what change would matter to you and by when. Desire usually starts to change at about three weeks and levels off by about six; a first review at about eight weeks catches most of the effect. (Saad et al., 2011)

When to stop

Guidelines advise reassessing within 12 months and stopping if there is no improvement. (ACP, 2020) An early review is not an automatic stopping rule; safety monitoring follows its own schedule.

What a lab number can and cannot show

A higher testosterone level shows that the drug is absorbed. It does not show that the man feels better. Desire also depends on relationships, fatigue, mood and sleep.

What this means

Forecast the change, act, and verify it by the outcome the treatment was started for.

The level is a marker. Desire is the result.

03

What has been shown in people?

A small but consistent improvement in desire across placebo-controlled trials. Erections: little or none.

The green verdict rests on the whole body of trials, not on one secondary endpoint.

Key trials

TRAVERSE Sexual Function1,161 men, 45–80, up to 2 yearsLow libido, two tests below 300 ng/dL, cardiovascular risk; 1.62% gel. Desire better than placebo at 6, 12 and 24 months. Sexual activity improved at 6 and 12 months. Erectile function: no difference. (Pencina et al., 2024)Desire was a secondary endpoint; many men dropped out; placebo also improved.
TTrials, sexual function470 men, 65+, 12 monthsLow libido, testosterone below 275 ng/dL; 1% gel. Desire +2.93 points versus placebo; activity improved; erectile function improved slightly. (Snyder et al., 2016; Cunningham et al., 2016)Only men aged 65+.
T4DM, secondary analysis1,007 men, 50–74, 2 yearsMen with a large waist and impaired glucose tolerance or new type 2 diabetes, on a lifestyle program; injections every 3 months. Desire improved by 2 points or more in 32% versus 22%. (Wittert et al., 2025)Not selected for hypogonadism; the threshold is not independently validated.
Brock 2016715 men, 19–92, 12 weeksTestosterone below 300 ng/dL with low desire or energy; 2% solution. Desire improved. (Brock et al., 2016)Short; the main endpoint was the hormone level; no separate result for younger men.

Pooled evidence

A meta-analysis commissioned by the Endocrine Society, four trials at low risk of bias, 1,779 men: a small but significant effect on desire; no effect on energy or mood. (Ponce et al., 2018) An individual-participant analysis of 17 trials and 3,431 men (median age 67): the improvement in sexual function did not depend on age, obesity, diabetes or baseline testosterone. (TestES, 2023–2024)

Professional positions

The International Consultation on Sexual Medicine (2024): testosterone to improve libido in men with hypogonadism, strong recommendation, high-quality evidence. (Rastrelli et al., 2025) The Endocrine Society: clear benefit when hypogonadism is caused by disease of the testes, pituitary or hypothalamus; long-term safety, including prostate cancer, not established. (Bhasin et al., 2018; Endocrine Society, 2026) The American College of Physicians: discuss treatment with men who have age-related low testosterone and sexual dysfunction; reassess within 12 months; do not start for energy, vitality, physical or cognitive function. (ACP, 2020)

The limits of this review. This is a targeted audit of the sources, read in full text where available, not an exhaustive systematic review of every database. Our ratings of evidence certainty are editorial judgments based on GRADE principles, not a completed formal grading. Outside the scope: testosterone for energy, mood, muscle, cognition or anti-aging; testosterone in women.
What this means

Several placebo-controlled trials point the same way: testosterone improves low desire in men with confirmed hypogonadism. The average effect is small. A meaningful change comes for a share of men, not for all.

Proven. Modest. Not for everyone.

Evidence certainty: moderate for the effect on desire; low to moderate for the share with a meaningful change; very low for men aged 35–44.

04

What does a mistake cost?

Fertility first. Then blood counts, heart rhythm and clotting. Long-term safety not fully known.

The two common mistakes are treating a man who does not have hypogonadism, and treating a man who plans to have children.

Fertility

Suppressed sperm production is an established effect. In contraceptive studies, 157 of 271 men stopped producing sperm. (WHO Task Force, 1990) Recovery after short-term use: 67% by 6 months, 90% by 12, 100% by 24 months. (Liu et al., 2006) After therapy in clinical practice, 46 of 66 men with very low or absent sperm counts reached the target after fertility treatment; recovery was slower in older men and after longer use. (Kohn et al., 2017)

Heart, blood and bones

In TRAVERSE, testosterone was not worse than placebo for major cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.96; 0.78–1.17), but atrial fibrillation, arrhythmia needing intervention, acute kidney injury, pulmonary embolism and fractures were more frequent. (Lincoff et al., 2023; Snyder et al., 2024) Red blood cells rise: the risk of erythrocytosis is about eight times higher than with placebo. (Ponce et al., 2018) In an earlier trial in men aged 65+, non-calcified coronary plaque grew more on testosterone; the clinical meaning is unknown. (Budoff et al., 2017)

Who should not start, or should start with caution

Untreated prostate or breast cancer; a heart attack or stroke in the last four months. With cardiovascular disease, past blood clots or heart failure: caution and hematocrit monitoring. A family history of blood clots should be discussed first. (EAU, 2026; EMAS, 2023)

What this means

These risks are the reason for the red clip. The benefit is real; selecting the right men and monitoring them is what keeps it worth the cost.

Evidence certainty: high for erythrocytosis and suppressed sperm production; moderate for the TRAVERSE signals; low for full recovery of fertility after years of use; not established beyond about three years.

Who paid for the evidence?

Funders and author ties of the key studies, named on both sides.

Much of the testosterone evidence was paid for, at least in part, by manufacturers. That does not make the results wrong. It is a reason to look at the design, and at the independent data. We name funders for the studies behind this verdict, whether they support it or not.

Manufacturer-led

TRAVERSE and its sub-studies. A consortium of testosterone manufacturers led by AbbVie; coordinated by the Cleveland Clinic. In the sexual function paper, three authors are AbbVie employees and shareholders.

The HIS-Q scale used in TRAVERSE. Developed with the participation of AbbVie employees.

Brock 2016. Sponsored by Eli Lilly; company representatives took part in the study.

Saad 2011. The first author is a Bayer employee.

Public or mixed

TTrials and Wang 2018. Funded by the National Institute on Aging and other NIH institutes. AbbVie also provided funding, the gel and placebo; according to the authors, it had no role in design, conduct, analysis or publication.

T4DM. Australia's NHMRC, Bayer, Eli Lilly, the University of Adelaide and others.

Ponce 2018. Commissioned by the Endocrine Society.

How we read this

The NIH-led TTrials point in the same direction as the manufacturer-led data. That is the strongest reason to trust the direction of the effect, while keeping its size modest.
