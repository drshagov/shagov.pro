VERDICT  006  Procedures

Microplastic detox. Can you really clear it from your body?

Microplastics are found in human blood and tissues. Clinics now sell blood cleansing to remove them, at about £10,000 for a single session in London, often as a course. Is there evidence that any commercial method clears microplastics from the body?

SHAGOV  |  VERDICT.Not justified.Issued · 24.11.2026

No commercial detox has been shown to produce a lasting reduction of microplastics in the body of healthy adults. On current evidence, paying for this purpose is not justified. This is not a finding against the established medical uses of apheresis.

They promise to clean your body. They measure your blood.

Promise checked
"We can clear microplastics from your body." Most often sold as blood cleansing, and also as test-and-retest programs, supplements and sauna protocols.
Checked for
Healthy adults with no medical indication for apheresis. Separately: what is known for people aged 35–50. "Lasting" means a reduction confirmed by a repeat measurement some time after treatment ends, not only immediately after it.
What this verdict does not mean

That apheresis does not work as a medical treatment. Plasma exchange has recognised indications and can be necessary care. This verdict does not touch those uses.

That nothing changes in the blood. In some studies, particle counts fell immediately after a procedure. Those results are shown in full below, with their limits.

That microplastics are harmless, or that they are a proven cause of disease. Neither is established, and this review does not assess their toxicity.

That physicians who offer these procedures are acting in bad faith. This review judges a claim, not people.

Not justified does not mean proven impossible. This verdict concerns one specific promise. It is dated and will be reconsidered when stronger evidence becomes available.

Take this with you

What to do

First, plainly: on current evidence, paying for a procedure to clear microplastics from your body is not justified.

What follows is practical. It is not a protocol for removing microplastics, and it does not claim a health benefit.

Fewer particles in a blood test is not proof of a cleaner body.

  1. Reduce exposure, instead of buying a promise of removal.Where tap water is safe to drink, use it instead of routinely buying water in single-use plastic bottles. Avoid heating food and drinks in plastic containers when you don't need to. (Qian et al., 2024; Hussain et al., 2023) These are sensible ways to reduce possible exposure. They are not a proven way to remove microplastics already in the body.
  2. Don't undergo blood cleansing for microplastics alone.Therapeutic plasma exchange has recognised medical indications. A high number on a commercial microplastics test is not one of them. If you have a condition for which apheresis is used, discuss it with a specialist: benefits and risks are weighed for that condition, not for a detox promise. (ASFA, 2026)
  3. Don't let a test result make the decision.There is no accepted clinical norm for microplastics in blood and no "time to act" threshold. The result does not guide a medical decision. It can, however, become the argument for selling the next procedure.
Three questions before you pay

What exactly am I paying for, and how will you show that my body, not just my blood test, is cleaner?

  1. What exactly do you remove, and how do you prove it?Ask which method measures the result: a dye that stains particles, or chemical identification of the polymer. Ask what size range is counted, and whether the tubes, bags and needles were checked for plastic.
  2. Were results independently rechecked after the procedure or course ended? How much later?A measurement taken minutes after a procedure shows what happened to the blood at that moment. It says nothing about whether the change lasted.
  3. What proves that my body is cleaner, not just my blood test?Blood is not the whole body. No validated clinical test measures total microplastic load today. If the answer is "your blood count went down", that is not an answer to this question.
01 · The Promise

A medical procedure. Now sold as a detox.

Microplastics are tiny plastic particles. Scientists have detected them in human blood and in tissues. Concern is real, and so is the market that has grown around it.

Apheresis, the family of procedures that filter or exchange blood plasma, has been used in medicine for decades for specific conditions. Removing microplastics is not one of its recognised indications.

Public figures turned it into a trend. Orlando Bloom showed a plasma-cleansing session in London on Instagram. Bryan Johnson reported an 85% drop in microplastics in his semen and a blood count falling from 15 particles to 1, while using sauna, plasma exchange and reduced plastic exposure at the same time. One person, several interventions: which one worked cannot be established. His company, Blueprint, sells a microplastics blood test for $135.

One promise, four forms

Machine blood cleansing is the main category. Around it: testing cycles (measure, treat, measure again), supplements that promise to "bind and eliminate", and sauna protocols that promise to "sweat it out". Many offers quietly switch from plastic particles to plastic chemicals such as BPA or phthalates. Removing a dissolved chemical is not the same as removing a particle.

The promise also expands as it is sold. Some providers say only that they reduce particles detected in blood. Others promise to clear the body. Some go further, to energy, sleep and clarity. This verdict checks the middle step: clearing the body.

What it costs

Plasma exchange is advertised at $5,000–15,000 per procedure, in courses of three to six. A sorption-column session in London costs about £10,000. Double filtration of plasma is offered at comparable prices in Dubai and Bangkok. Test-treat-retest programs run to hundreds of dollars per cycle; consumer blood tests, about $135. Prices change; this is an illustration, not a market index. Clinics are not named: the verdict concerns the claim, not the seller.

02 · The Chain

Four steps of the sales story. Where it breaks.

03 · Same Word

"Blood cleansing". Three different machines.

The word is the same. The technology is not, and results from one method cannot be transferred to another.

The celebrity procedure

The famous one has the least data.

What was shown on InstagramSorption

The procedure Orlando Bloom shared in London uses an adsorbing column. Its published clinical reference comes from critically ill patients with COVID-19, not from microplastic removal.

What was studiedPlasma exchange

The only published human study of a procedure for microplastics tested a different method, run by a different company.

Same word ≠ same evidence.

The strongest version of the claim

We judge the promise in its strongest form: plasma exchange, the most studied method, in the hands of the company that published the key study. Even there, what was shown is a change in blood immediately after a procedure, in both directions. The questions about the body and about durability remain.

The best-studied method settles what happens in the blood that day. It does not settle what happens in the body.

04 · Why Now

More data. A clearer limit.

Until 2026, microplastic detox was a promise with almost no human data. This year brought the first published studies. They show both what happens and where the evidence stops.

  • May 2026: the first published human study of plasma exchange for microplastics. Counts fell after high starting levels and rose after low ones. The authors found particle signals in the system's own saline bags and tubing. (Weinstein et al., 2026)
  • 2026: small studies of double filtration, with results going in both directions. (Bornstein et al., 2026)
  • September 2026: in mice, particles accumulated inside immune cells of the liver and stayed there. A procedure that works on plasma does not directly reach particles inside cells. (Makdissi et al., 2026)
  • No study measured the whole body before and after any detox, and no peer-reviewed study rechecked blood after a course ended.

A research question can stay scientifically interesting while paying for the promised result becomes unjustified. Scientific uncertainty is orange. The commercial promise, sold as settled, is red.

More research does not automatically mean more evidence for the promise.

The specialists' view

The people who run these machines said: not yet.

As a medical treatmentYes

The American Society for Apheresis publishes guidelines for recognised indications of therapeutic apheresis. (ASFA, 2026)

To remove microplasticsNo recommendation

In its 2026 guidelines, the society reviewed apheresis for longevity, anti-aging and removal of pollutants, including microplastics, and did not issue recommendations, citing insufficient data on tissue clearance and clinical outcomes. (ASFA, 2026)

Medical use ≠ detox proof.

The verdict is not borrowed from a professional society. It follows from the human evidence, the outcome that is promised and the price of error.

Evidence at a glance

What was promised, and what was shown.

Implied promise
A commercial procedure clears microplastics from your body, and the effect lasts.
Proof needed
A validated measurement, a control group, an assessment of the body (or at least more than one compartment), and a repeat measurement some time after treatment ends. A change in one blood sample would not be enough.
Evidence
Changes in blood immediately after a procedure, in both directions, in uncontrolled studies. A small change in blood polymer mass after a supplement. No peer-reviewed delayed recheck. No whole-body measurement. No health outcomes.
Trade-offs
An invasive procedure with mostly mild but real side effects, loss of plasma proteins, possible plastic from consumables, and thousands of dollars, for an unproven endpoint.
The detail

Four questions. Open any one.

01

What is promised, and to whom?

A body cleared of microplastics. Sold to healthy adults, often as a course or an annual repeat.

The promise, as checked: a commercial method can clear microplastics from the body of a healthy adult, and the reduction lasts.

How the promise sounds

Some providers phrase it narrowly: reducing circulating particles. Others offer a dedicated "microplastic removal" session of 60–90 minutes for body cleansing, cellular health and energy. Device makers describe "deep detoxification that would take years, in two hours". Many sell annual repeats or test-treat-retest cycles.

Who was actually studied

Plasma exchangeOne study114 patients of functional-medicine and wellness clinics, aged 18–95, mean 55.8. (Weinstein et al., 2026)Not a group of healthy adults. No separate result for ages 35–50.
Double filtrationSmall seriesPatients with chronic illness; microplastic results in groups of two to four people. (Bornstein et al., 2026)Not healthy adults.
ChitosanTwo small studiesHealthy adults: 10 in one study; 21 aged 18–53 in the other. (Casella et al., 2025; Cornelli et al., 2026)No separate result for ages 35–50.
What this means

The promise is made to healthy adults. The procedure data come from patients. The group that is promised and the groups that were studied do not match.

02

How can the result be checked?

In blood, partly. In the body, not with any validated clinical method today.

"There is less plastic in me" can mean four different things. They are often merged into one.

Four levels of claim

1. DetectionA plastic-like signalThe sample contains something compatible with plastic. Reliability depends on the method and its contamination controls.
2. QuantificationHow many, in this sampleThis volume of blood contains this many particles, or this mass of polymer. Valid only for that method, polymer list and size range.
3. ComparisonBefore and afterThere were fewer particles after the procedure. Needs the same method, contamination checks and a control; a before-and-after change alone does not prove cause.
4. The bodyTotal load over timeThe body as a whole holds less plastic, and that lasts. No accepted, clinically validated method measures this today.

The test behind the key study

The plasma-exchange study used a commercial test: 100 µL of dried blood, stained with a fluorescent dye (Nile Red), with suspected particles counted. The dye is not specific to plastic, and the method's full validation is proprietary; no independent chemical identification was done on the same samples. (Weinstein et al., 2026) Interlaboratory comparisons show that microplastic results still vary widely between methods and labs. (Interlaboratory comparison, 2025)

Detection is not quantification

Infrared and Raman methods can identify certain polymers. Pyrolysis–GC/MS estimates polymer mass but loses particle count and size, and in blood it can be unreliable for some polymers. Fluorescent dyes can stain non-plastic residues. Counts and mass are different quantities and cannot be added up. (Rauert et al., 2025; Nardella et al., 2026) A 2026 quality audit of 133 datasets from 101 human studies found none that met every essential element of its quality-control framework. That does not make the findings false; it means confidence has to be shown study by study. (Lane et al., 2026)

Plastic can enter the sample

Needles, tubes, bags and lancets are made of plastic. Disposable medical devices can release particles. (Medical devices study, 2025) In the plasma-exchange study, the authors found particle signals in saline bags and in the system's tubing.

What "lasting" means here

Before reviewing the evidence, we defined it as a reduction confirmed by a repeat measurement some time after the procedure or course ended. We did not invent a minimum of a week or a month. No peer-reviewed clinical study met even that definition.

And the whole body?

Models of total body load give estimates that differ by hundreds of times, depending on data and assumptions. A blood count after treatment cannot tell anyone what share of the body's plastic was removed. (Koelmans et al., 2026)

What this means

A blood count after a procedure can be checked, within the limits of the method. Whether the body is cleaner cannot be checked today by any validated clinical test, by us or by the clinic.

03

What has been shown in people?

Changes in blood immediately after a procedure, in both directions. Nothing about the body, durability or health.

There is a real experimental signal, and we show it in full, even where it comes from a company that sells the procedure. What it does not show is a cleaner body.

Plasma exchange · no control group

Groups were formed from a single starting measurement, so regression to the mean is likely, though not proven to explain everything. The paper reports 114 patients and 174 procedures, while the listed repeat procedures add up to 156, a discrepancy that needs the authors' clarification. No measurement was made after the procedure day. (Weinstein et al., 2026)

Double filtration · single-digit groups

Chemical analysis4 patientsPolyethylene fell in all four; PVC fell in three; polypropylene rose in two; polystyrene rose in one. (Bornstein et al., 2026)Mixed directions, no control, immediately after.
Dye count4 patientsAbout 70% fewer stained objects. Not statistically significant; the dye does not confirm plastic.

Chitosan · two small studies

Casella et al., 202510 healthy adultsMore particles in stool after a meal with chitosan (656 → 965). (Casella et al., 2025)Shows more food particles leaving through the gut; not removal of plastic already in blood or tissues.
Cornelli et al., 202611 vs 10, 15 daysPolymer mass in blood 1.84 → 1.34 µg/mL on chitosan; 1.87 → 1.85 on placebo. Plastic remained in every participant. (Cornelli et al., 2026)Not randomised; no proper between-group comparison; no measurement after stopping.

Sauna

No controlled study shows microplastic particles leaving the body through sweat. Plastic chemicals such as BPA have been found in sweat; those are dissolved substances, not particles.

Delayed rechecks

Delayed blood measurements, up to months after treatment, appear only in a commercial provider's presentation, marked as pending publication, with procedures and supplements continuing during follow-up. They are not independently verified. (MDLifeSpan, 2025)

The limits of this review. This is a targeted audit of the sources, read in full text where available, not an exhaustive systematic review. Our ratings of evidence quality are editorial judgments. Outside the scope: recognised medical indications for apheresis, the toxicity of microplastics, and microplastic tests as a separate product.
What this means

Four statements, kept apart. Blood counts can change immediately after a procedure: shown, in both directions. The change lasts after the course: not shown. The body is cleaner: not measured. Health improves: not studied.

A change in the blood. An unmeasured body.

Evidence quality: low for changes in blood; absent for clearing the body. Ages 35–50: no direct evidence.

04

What does a mistake cost?

An invasive procedure, real if mostly mild side effects, and thousands of dollars, for a result that was not measured.

For recognised indications, apheresis is routine clinical care. The question here is narrower: is it justified to put a healthy person through it for a promise of detox?

Almost all risk data come from patients, not healthy people. Rates cannot be transferred directly in either direction.

0.3–0.7%serious adverse events per plasma-exchange procedure, in large series of patients
2–3 daysfor fibrinogen to recover after an exchange; immunoglobulins take longer
4.4 → 14.4the average in the largest group after a procedure, with plastic found in the system's own consumables

1. The procedure

In large series of plasma exchange in patients, adverse events were frequent but mostly mild; serious events occurred in 0.3% and 0.7% of procedures. (Coirier et al., 2022; Bramlage et al., 2009) The procedure needs vascular access, anticoagulation and fluid replacement; reported problems include low blood pressure, citrate-related low calcium, allergic reactions and access complications, with infection and clotting more likely through a central catheter. These rates come from patients treated for real diseases and cannot be turned into a personal risk for a healthy adult.

2. Plasma proteins

When plasma is replaced with albumin, fibrinogen recovers in two to three days and immunoglobulins more slowly. With frequent sessions, levels can fall progressively. (Wood & Jacobs, 1986; Orlin & Berkman, 1980)

3. Plastic from the system

Saline bags and tubing can be a source of particles or of measurement signal. This is a possibility to account for, not an established dose to the patient.

4. Supplements

Chitosan carries a small but real cost: caution with shellfish-derived products in people with seafood allergy, a published case of interaction with warfarin, and no safety data in pregnancy. (UK COT; Case report, 2007)

5. The benefit is not established

Observational studies link microplastics to some diseases; one found more cardiovascular events in people with plastic in carotid plaque. Association is not cause. (Marfella et al., 2024) No detox study measured symptoms, disease or survival. This is context, not a condition of the verdict.

6. Money, time and attention

Thousands of dollars per procedure, courses and "annual maintenance", while daily exposure from food, water and air continues unchanged.

What this means

The main cost is not a complication. It is money, time and attention spent on a result that cannot be established, while the one step everyone agrees on, reducing exposure, costs little.

The cost is real. The promised result is unmeasured.

$

Who paid for the evidence?

Funders and author ties of the key studies, named on both sides.

Most human data on microplastic removal come from companies that sell the procedure or the device. That does not make the results wrong. It is a reason to look closely at the design and to ask for independent replication.

Weinstein et al., 2026 (plasma exchange). Seven co-authors are affiliated with Circulate Health, which offers plasma exchange; the last author is its co-founder and CEO. The other authors are from the clinics that collected the data. The test used is commercial.

Bornstein et al., 2026 (double filtration). The first author reports advisory roles with Ayus, the device manufacturer, and other equipment makers. Co-authors include Ayus employees and participants of Proxima Health.

Cornelli et al., 2026 (chitosan). The authors declare no conflicts. The supplier of the product is not named.

Delayed rechecks. Presented by a commercial plasma-exchange provider; unpublished.

Consumer blood test. Sold by Blueprint, Bryan Johnson's company, for $135.

We apply the same scrutiny to studies that support our verdict and those that challenge it. A financial relationship is not evidence that a finding is false. Nor does declaring a conflict resolve every question about independence.

No commercial detox has been shown to produce a lasting reduction of microplastics in the body of healthy adults. On current evidence, paying for this purpose is not justified. This is not a finding against the established medical uses of apheresis.

Microplastics are a real subject of environmental and medical research. But the seriousness of a problem does not prove that the solution being sold works.

Evidence reviewed: 14 November 2026. The verdict will be reconsidered if convincing new evidence emerges: for example, controlled studies with validated measurement and repeat testing after treatment ends.
