SHAGOV | VERDICT.  003  Lipoprotein(a)

Lp(a). One test for every adult?

Cholesterol gets checked dozens of times in a lifetime. Lp(a)? Often not even once. One test that can change how heart attack and stroke risk is assessed.

The claim · The evidence · The price of error

Yes. Every adult should have Lp(a) measured at least once. The test can reveal an inherited cardiovascular risk that a standard lipid panel misses. If it is elevated, it can inform earlier, more intensive management of LDL cholesterol and other modifiable risk factors.

The claim we tested
Every adult should have Lp(a) measured at least once. It can reveal an inherited risk of heart attack and stroke that a standard lipid panel does not show.
What this verdict does not mean

That the test itself prevents heart attacks or strokes. It improves the assessment of risk. Prevention is what lowers it.

That every high result calls for medication. The decision depends on overall risk, not on one number.

Whether lowering Lp(a) itself prevents heart attacks and strokes is a separate question. It will get its own VERDICT. This one covers a single question: should every adult be tested once.

Take this with you

What to do

Ask for a separate test: lipoprotein(a), Lp(a). A standard lipid panel does not include it. One measurement is usually enough for life.

No fasting is needed for Lp(a). Ideally, take it when you are well: not during an acute illness and, if possible, not during pregnancy. Keep the result with its date, units and laboratory.

Do not put it off if close relatives had an early heart attack or stroke, if you have familial high cholesterol, or if a relative is known to have high Lp(a).

The goal is not a good-looking number. It is a better-informed plan for your heart and blood vessels.

Three questions before the test
  1. What units will the result be in?nmol/L is preferred. If the lab only reports mg/dL, take the test anyway. Do not convert between the two yourself.
  2. Who will explain the result together with my overall risk?Lp(a) is read alongside LDL, blood pressure, blood sugar, smoking and family history, not on its own.
  3. If I am offered regular repeat testing, why?Most people need it only once. A repeat makes sense in specific situations, listed below.
01 · The Marker

What Lp(a) is — and why a standard test misses it.

Lp(a) is a cholesterol-carrying particle similar to LDL, with one extra protein attached: apolipoprotein(a).

Its level is set mainly by your genes. According to the European guidelines, more than 90% of it is inherited. (ESC/EAS, 2025) Diet and exercise usually change Lp(a) itself very little. That does not make them less useful: they still work on LDL, blood pressure and the other risk factors you can change.

A standard lipid panel measures total cholesterol, LDL, HDL and triglycerides. It does not give an Lp(a) value. That takes a separate test. (ACC/AHA, 2026)

Levels differ between people by orders of magnitude. Elevated Lp(a) is a causal risk factor for atherosclerotic events and for calcific aortic valve stenosis. The link is strongest for coronary events. For ischemic stroke it is also established, but weaker on average.

So a person can have normal cholesterol at every check-up and still carry an inherited risk that none of those tests could show.

The scale of the problem

Heart attacks and strokes caused an estimated 17 million deaths in 2022. This is our own calculation from WHO data: 19.8 million cardiovascular deaths, about 85% of them from heart attack and stroke. (WHO, 2025) It is the background, not a number attributed to Lp(a). Most of these deaths have many causes.

In the United States, a database covering about 300 million patient records found Lp(a) tests recorded for 728,550 people between 2015 and 2024. The authors estimate this at about 0.2% of the US population. It is not a population survey, and it may miss tests done outside the network. (Ramsis et al., 2025)

What this means

Elevated Lp(a) is common, largely inherited and invisible on a routine cholesterol test. Testing for it is rare.

Evidence quality: prevalence — high. Testing rates — estimates with different denominators.

02 · The Evidence

Three checks behind the verdict.

The claim stands only if three things hold. Elevated Lp(a) must add risk on its own. One measurement must be enough for most people. And the result must be able to change what is done, with measures that work.

Check 1 · Risk

The claimElevated Lp(a) is an independent risk factor for heart attack and ischemic stroke, including when LDL is normal.

The check: Confirmed. Evidence quality: high.

Erqou et al., 200936 prospective studies · 126,634 peopleAfter adjusting for lipids and other factors, each standard deviation of Lp(a) was linked to higher risk of coronary heart disease (×1.13) and ischemic stroke (×1.10). The link is continuous, independent and moderate. (ERFC)Moderate on average across the population.
Kamstrup et al., 2009Genetic studyGenetically higher Lp(a): ×1.22 risk of heart attack for each doubling of the level. This supports a causal role. (Kamstrup et al.)Genetics shows cause. It does not show what happens if the level is lowered with drugs.
UK Biobank, 2021460,506 people · 11.2 yearsRisk rose in a straight line: ×1.11 for every 50 nmol/L. 12.2% of people without heart disease had 150 nmol/L or more, with about 1.5 times the risk. (Patel et al.)Observational data.
Bhatia et al., 20256 statin trials · 27,658 peopleEven in the group with the lowest LDL achieved, Lp(a) above 50 mg/dL carried higher risk: ×1.38. Lowering LDL does not fully cancel the risk from Lp(a). (Bhatia et al.)Lp(a) was measured with different methods in different trials.
Women's Health Study, 202427,939 women · 30 yearsThe top fifth of Lp(a) had 33% higher risk. One baseline measurement predicted risk three decades ahead. (Ridker et al.)Women only.
Mayo Clinic, 202635,622 adults · median age 46Men aged 18–50 and women aged 18–60 without diagnosed coronary disease. Lp(a) of 50 mg/dL or more was associated with premature coronary artery disease: ×1.40 overall, ×1.67 in people with LDL below 100 mg/dL. (Bawahab & Vasile)Retrospective. People referred for testing, not a random sample.

Why this matters at 35–50

The guidelines recommend a one-time measurement for all adults, including this age group. (ACC/AHA, 2026; ESC/EAS, 2025) The Mayo Clinic study adds direct data on younger and middle-aged adults: elevated Lp(a) was linked to coronary disease that started early. So the case for this age group is not just borrowed from older people.

What is still missing: a randomized trial showing that testing everyone in this age group reduces heart attacks and strokes. The practical benefit rests on the general evidence for prevention and on the guidelines.

What this means

Elevated Lp(a) adds risk independently and causally, including when LDL is low. On average the effect is moderate; it grows as the level rises. It is not "the main risk factor". It is an important, inherited one that a routine cholesterol test cannot see.

A relative risk of ×1.4 does not mean a 40% chance of a heart attack.

Check 2 · Once

The claimOne measurement is usually enough. A repeat is needed for some people, not for all.

The check: Confirmed, with exceptions.

The guidelines. The American guideline: measure at least once in every adult; repeating is usually unnecessary because the level is mostly inherited. (ACC/AHA, 2026) The European guideline: at least once in a lifetime, ideally with the first lipid test. (ESC/EAS, 2025) The US National Lipid Association: at least once in all adults, with a repeat possibly justified, especially after an intermediate result. (NLA, 2026)

The data. In UK Biobank, about 12,000 people were measured twice, about 4.4 years apart. The two results correlated at 0.94. Of those who started low, only 0.2% moved to the high category. Of those in the intermediate range, 29.5% moved up and 9.0% moved down. (Ghouse et al., 2025)

In 715 people measured on one platform about three years apart, 91.6% stayed in their original category. (Koller et al., 2025) In Norway, across 922,304 measurements, first and last results correlated at 0.94–0.96. Most of the variation came from the laboratory system, not from the body. (Vajeeston et al., 2026, conference abstract)

Concentration can change without changing the category. In a Polish cohort of cardiology patients, 44.3% had a change of 20% or more, yet 93.8% stayed in their original risk category. (STAR-Lp(a), 2025) In a German university clinic, 11.3% changed category over a median of 13 months; many were patients with heart disease, and some changed treatment. (Seidel et al., 2026)

Some situations do shift the level. After menopause, women as a group have higher Lp(a): about 27% in a Danish population. That is a group comparison, not a guaranteed rise for each woman. (Copenhagen General Population Study, 2022) Pregnancy, some kidney diseases and inflammation can also change it, in different directions and by different amounts. (EAS consensus, 2022)

mg/dL and nmol/L

Lp(a) is reported in two units. There is no exact formula to convert one into the other for a given person: the ratio depends on the size of the particle. nmol/L is preferred. A result in mg/dL is still valid. Read it in the units the lab used.

What this means

For most adults, one good measurement is enough. A repeat helps when the result is near a threshold and could change a decision, or when the first test was taken in unusual circumstances.

Evidence quality: high for stability in categories; a repeat is a decision, not a routine.

Check 3 · Action

The claimA high Lp(a) shows who should manage other risk factors earlier and more closely. Those measures are proven, and they work when Lp(a) is high.

The check: Confirmed. There is no direct trial of "test → change → fewer events", and a diagnostic test does not need one.

A. The result changes the risk estimate

In more than 314,000 people without heart disease, Lp(a) of 125 nmol/L or more meant about 30% higher risk. Adding Lp(a) to the PREVENT risk calculator modestly improved prediction, most of all in people at low estimated risk. (Bhatia et al., 2025) The European guideline uses Lp(a) above 105 nmol/L to refine risk in people at moderate risk or near a treatment threshold. (ESC/EAS, 2025)

B. The result changes decisions, for some people

When overall risk is borderline or intermediate, Lp(a) of 125 nmol/L or more is a reason to start or intensify statin therapy. For people with low overall risk, one high Lp(a) does not mean pills. (ACC/AHA, 2026)

At 125 nmol/L or more, the American guideline calls for early, optimal control of modifiable risk factors, and recommends testing first-degree relatives. (ACC/AHA, 2026; NLA, 2026)

C. The chosen actions lower risk, also with high Lp(a)

In the JUPITER trial, rosuvastatin reduced cardiovascular events both in people with Lp(a) above the median and in those below it, with no difference in effect. Lp(a) remained a source of residual risk on the statin. This was a subgroup analysis of a randomized trial, not a trial of a testing strategy. (Khera et al., 2014)

In 6,676 adults without heart disease, good cardiovascular health on seven basic measures (Life's Simple 7) was linked to lower risk at every level of Lp(a). Among people with Lp(a) of 50 mg/dL or more, the risk was about a third of that with poor scores. These are associations from an observational study. (Razavi et al., 2025)

The benefit of lowering LDL, controlling blood pressure and not smoking is established by randomized trials. It is the common base of prevention.

What this means

The result sharpens the estimate of risk, changes decisions for some people, and points to measures with proven benefit. No trial has tested the whole chain from test to fewer events, and none is required to judge the value of a diagnostic test.

One number does not dictate treatment. It helps inform prevention.

03 · The Price of Error

Five ways to get it wrong — with or without the test.

  1. Not measuring itElevated Lp(a) does not show on a standard lipid panel. A person with normal cholesterol may carry an extra risk that neither they nor their doctor knows about. Overall risk is underestimated. Prevention is decided without important information. Relatives do not learn that they should be tested. We cannot say that skipping the test causes a specific number of heart attacks or strokes.
  2. Taking a high result as a diagnosisLp(a) is a risk factor, not a diagnosis of atherosclerosis and not a personal forecast. Absolute risk depends on age, LDL, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes and family history. A high result can cause anxiety. In an online survey of 1,001 people who reported elevated Lp(a), 71.7% had minimal anxiety and 70.7% did not regret being tested; this was a self-selected sample. (Eversole et al., 2025) Patients with heart disease worried about high results but found testing acceptable and were motivated to reduce their risk. (INTERASPIRE, 2025) Anxiety is a possible cost, not an inevitable one.
  3. Taking a low result as a guaranteeLow Lp(a) does not add the risk that comes with elevated Lp(a). It does not mean zero cardiovascular risk, and it does not cancel the risk of high LDL, high blood pressure, diabetes or smoking. It is not a reason to stop prevention or treatment.
  4. Measuring or comparing it wronglyThe lab method, the units and your state of health all matter. Converting mg/dL to nmol/L with a fixed factor is not valid. Near a threshold, different methods can put the same person in different categories.
  5. Turning a risk into a shopping listA high Lp(a) on its own is not a general reason for medication, extra imaging, invasive procedures or regular lab monitoring. These bring costs, overdiagnosis and side effects of unneeded treatment, and they distract from prevention with proven benefit. Be careful with offers to "fix" a lab number without evidence that this improves health outcomes.

The cost of the test itself

One blood draw, usually together with a lipid panel. The price depends on the country, the lab and how care is paid for. The physical risks are those of any routine blood test.

What this means

The balance favors testing. The benefit is clearest when the result is used to assess overall risk, not as a diagnosis on its own and not as an automatic reason to treat.

04 · The Test

How to take it and how to read it.

Who

Every adult, at least once, whatever their usual cholesterol. Especially without delay if relatives had an early heart attack or stroke, if there is familial high cholesterol, or if a close relative is known to have high Lp(a).

How to take it

  1. Ask for a separate test: "Lipoprotein(a), Lp(a)". A standard lipid panel does not replace it.
  2. Prefer a lab with a validated method that reports in nmol/L. If only mg/dL is available, take the test anyway.
  3. No fasting is needed for Lp(a). Other tests taken at the same time may need it. (ACC/AHA, 2026)
  4. Take it when your health is stable; if possible, not during pregnancy or an acute inflammation. These are not strict prohibitions.
  5. Keep the result, the units, the date and the lab. Do not convert the units yourself.
  6. A genetic test is not needed for a routine risk assessment.

How to read it

below 75LowA meaningful increase in risk from Lp(a) is unlikely. Your other risk factors still matter.
75–124IntermediateConsider it as part of overall risk. In some situations, a repeat test may be considered.
125 and aboveElevatedA reason to assess overall risk more carefully and to control modifiable risk factors earlier.
250 and aboveMarkedly elevatedAbout twice the relative risk compared with the median. Calls for an especially careful individual assessment.

Lp(a) in nmol/L. NLA 2024 and 2026, ACC/AHA 2026. Risk rises continuously; there is no jump at a threshold. The European guideline uses above 50 mg/dL (about 105 nmol/L) as a risk-enhancing factor; the US guidance uses 125 nmol/L or more. These are different accepted criteria, not one threshold written two ways. (NLA, 2024; NLA, 2026; ESC/EAS, 2025)

If it is high

  1. Look at overall risk, not one number: LDL (and non-HDL or ApoB if needed), blood pressure, blood sugar, smoking, family history, kidney function. Use a validated risk calculator for your age and country.
  2. Review prevention with your doctor: earlier and closer control of LDL and the other modifiable factors, with measures of proven benefit. A statin is decided by overall risk; a high Lp(a) with low overall risk does not mean an automatic prescription.
  3. Offer the test to first-degree relatives: parents, brothers, sisters, children. Timing for children — with a pediatrician.
  4. Monitor what you act on: LDL, blood pressure, smoking, blood sugar. Repeating Lp(a) to track diet, exercise or standard therapy is usually not needed.

If it is low

Keep the result and continue usual prevention based on overall risk. Low Lp(a) does not make high LDL, high blood pressure, smoking or diabetes safe.

When to repeat

Most people do not need to. Consider a repeat if:

  • the result is intermediate or near a threshold, and a clearer answer could change a decision;
  • the first test was taken during pregnancy or a major change in health;
  • since the test, menopause has occurred, kidney or thyroid function has changed markedly, or treatment has changed substantially — especially if the first result was intermediate;
  • there are doubts about the quality of the first test.

Three questions after the result

  1. Does my Lp(a) change the estimate of my overall and long-term risk?
  2. Should the control of my LDL, blood pressure and other factors become more intensive?
  3. Which of my relatives should check their Lp(a)?

The verdict

Yes. Every adult should have Lp(a) measured at least once. The test can reveal an inherited cardiovascular risk that a standard lipid panel misses. If it is elevated, it can inform earlier, more intensive management of LDL cholesterol and other modifiable risk factors.

PROVEN refers to the value of the test for assessing risk and making prevention decisions. It is not a promise that the test itself prevents a heart attack or stroke. Evidence reviewed: 17 November 2026. The verdict may change if convincing new evidence emerges.

Separate VERDICT

Should Lp(a) itself be lowered? Whether lowering Lp(a) prevents heart attacks and strokes is a different question. It is not covered here and will get its own VERDICT. For now, the main step is to assess overall risk and to control LDL, blood pressure and other factors earlier, together with your doctor.
